Why We Lost Our Fur

Humans are the naked ape: hairless, sweaty, and able to run in heat. The three leading explanations are thermoregulation, parasites and sexual selection, and the fossil evidence does not choose between them.

The facts

Humans have much sparser body hair than any other primate, with the same density of hair follicles but much finer, shorter hairs. The loss appears to predate the genus Homo, or at least to be early in it. Clothing is much later: the body louse, which lives in clothing, diverged from the head louse roughly 100,000 years ago, which gives a date for regular clothing.

The explanations

The thermoregulation hypothesis, developed by Bramble and Lieberman, links hairlessness to sweating and endurance running: a furred animal cannot dump heat fast enough to run long distances in the heat, and eccrine sweating requires exposed skin. The parasite hypothesis says fur is a habitat, and losing it reduced the load. The sexual-selection hypothesis says hairlessness was selected as a signal.

Each explains part of the pattern. None explains the timing, which is poorly constrained, and the fossil record does not preserve hair.

Where it stands

Documented: reduced hair density, eccrine sweating, and the louse date for clothing.

Well supported: thermoregulation, as at least a major pressure.

Plausible: parasite load and sexual selection as contributors.

Not established: a single cause or a reliable date for the loss.

The honest label: live debate — a real anatomical fact with three good explanations and no decisive test.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the question every child asks about humans, and because the answer is a set of competing pressures rather than a story — which is what most of evolutionary biology actually looks like.
