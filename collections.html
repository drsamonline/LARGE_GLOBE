The six labels

The labels are applied the same way across every page. Each dossier carries one of these six verdicts, and where six words cannot carry the finding it adds a hand-written note in place of the label — the verdict behind the note is printed in the panel at the top of the page. They describe the state of the evidence, not the plausibility of the story — which is why a beautiful thought experiment and a fraudulent artefact can both end up labelled "Fiction" and "Debunked" respectively.

  • Documented — the thing exists or happened, and is on the record.
  • Live debate — real evidence, real specialists, no settled answer.
  • Open problem — precisely stated, neither proved nor disproved; the status line gives how far it has been checked.
  • Fringe — no positive evidence, often unfalsifiable in practice.
  • Debunked — tested and failed, or the product of a known hoax.
  • Fiction — invented story, fan theory or internet lore, labelled as such rather than as a claim about the world.

How to read a label honestly

Two things follow from applying labels at this scale, and both are worth stating plainly rather than burying.

The labels are editorial. They are my reading of the published evidence, applied consistently, and arguable at the edges — especially where a fan theory is neither true nor false but an interpretation of a story.

A label is not a verdict on the person who believes it. Most of these ideas are interesting because they are almost right: the mistake is usually a step too far, not a fantasy from nothing. The reading guide sets out the ladder the labels are built on.

What comes next

The collections were submitted as lists. The work now is conversion: taking the rows that deserve it and giving them the full treatment — the claim, the strongest version of the argument for it, the evidence against, the sources, and a clear statement of what remains unknown. Twenty-eight are already live: the four starters below, ten dossiers from the cosmology chapter of the frontier index, and four modern scandals — Theranos, FTX, Pegasus and the Panama Papers.

Each conversion removes a row from the "unwritten" column and adds a page to the themes, the charts and the printable edition. Nothing is deleted from the indexes; the dossier simply becomes the place the title points.
