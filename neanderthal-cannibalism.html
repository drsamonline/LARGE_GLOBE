Neanderthal Cannibalism

Bones from several Neanderthal sites carry cut marks and fractures consistent with butchery. The evidence is direct, and the interpretation is not.

The evidence

Neanderthal remains from sites including Krapina in Croatia, El Sidrón in Spain, Moula-Guercy in France and Goyet in Belgium show cut marks, percussion fractures and burning consistent with the removal of flesh and marrow. At El Sidrón, the marks on a group of individuals are in the same places as those made in butchery.

The interpretation

Cannibalism is the straightforward reading, and it is accepted for several sites. What is argued is why: nutritional stress, ritual treatment of the dead, or something else. At some sites the pattern suggests the dead were processed like animals; at others, the marks are consistent with secondary burial practices.

The evidence is direct and unusual in archaeology — it is written on the bones — and the debate is about meaning rather than fact.

What is established

Documented: the cut marks, fractures and burning.

Documented: several sites across Europe.

Accepted: that the bodies were butchered.

Contested: the motive.

The label: documented.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is a claim about our closest relatives that the evidence supports, and because the uncomfortable part is the part that is documented.
