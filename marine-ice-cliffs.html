Marine Ice Cliffs

The claim that tall ice cliffs at the edge of Antarctica could collapse rapidly, raising sea level faster than models allow. Later work suggests the cliffs are stronger than the hypothesis assumed.

The hypothesis

The marine ice cliff instability hypothesis proposed that ice cliffs above about 100 metres cannot support themselves and collapse rapidly, exposing a taller cliff behind and producing a self-sustaining retreat. It was used to argue that Antarctica could contribute much more to sea level rise this century than models projected.

Maps, diagrams and images

The evidence

Observations of existing ice cliffs found that they are more stable than the hypothesis assumed, and modelling work from 2021 onwards found that the collapse mechanism is unlikely to operate at the required scale. The process is real but the catastrophic version is not supported by the data.

The hypothesis was a legitimate attempt to bound a tail risk, and its revision is what the literature looks like when a risk is downgraded.

Where it stands

Documented: calving and cliff retreat.

Contested: the instability mechanism at scale.

Revised down: after observational work.

Not excluded: as a local process.

The honest label: live debate.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is a case where a worrying hypothesis was tested against data and weakened, which is worth recording beside the cases where the worrying hypothesis was confirmed.
