Voice Identification

Recognising a voice is something people do well and can do badly under stress.

The methods

Voice comparison is done by earwitnesses, by phonetic experts and by software. Earwitness identification is documented as unreliable, with studies showing high error rates, particularly across languages and under stress.

Maps, diagrams and images

The state of the field

Forensic speaker comparison by experts has better performance than earwitnesses, and it is documented as variable and dependent on recording quality. Courts have treated it cautiously, and the field is working on validated methods.

Why an ear is not a fingerprint

The ear is good at recognising voices it knows and poor at identifying voices it does not. In studies where listeners hear a voice once and are asked to pick it out of a line-up of similar voices, accuracy falls sharply — and confidence rises anyway, which is the dangerous combination.

Automatic speaker recognition is a different instrument with a different error profile: it works well on clean recordings of sufficient length, and degrades with noise, compression and short samples. Telephone audio, which is what most evidence consists of, is exactly the case where the degradation is worst.

Forensic voice comparison therefore tends to be reported as a likelihood ratio rather than an identification, which is the honest form: how much more probable is this recording under one hypothesis than the other. A court that treats the output as an identification has misunderstood the instrument.
