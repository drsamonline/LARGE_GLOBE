The Shadow Biosphere

The proposal that life with a different biochemistry exists on Earth, undetected because our methods assume ours. It is a serious hypothesis with one famous failed test.

The proposal

A shadow biosphere would be life that shares the planet but not our biochemistry — a different genetic alphabet, different amino acids, different solvents. It would be invisible to standard assays, which look for DNA and proteins of the kind we know.

Maps, diagrams and images

The idea was developed by astrobiologists including Carol Cleland and Paul Davies as a way to think about the origin of life: if life began more than once, descendants of a different origin might still be here.

The test that failed

In 2010 a team reported a bacterium, GFAJ-1, from Mono Lake, that appeared to substitute arsenic for phosphorus in its DNA. It was the closest thing to a shadow-biosphere result, and it was heavily publicised. Within two years, two independent reanalyses found that the arsenic was not in the DNA and that the organism was simply tolerating arsenic while scavenging trace phosphorus.

No other candidate has survived. The hypothesis remains unfalsified in principle and unsupported in practice, and the methods that would find it are not obviously available.

Where it stands

Documented: the hypothesis, and the reasoning behind it.

Failed: the arsenic-life claim, on reanalysis.

Not found: any organism with a demonstrably different biochemistry.

Not excluded: in principle.

The honest label: fringe — a well-posed question with no positive evidence and one prominent retraction.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the archive's most respectable 'we might be missing something' claim, and because the arsenic story is a model of how a dramatic result is dismantled by careful chemistry.
