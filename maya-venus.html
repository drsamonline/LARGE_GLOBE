The Maya Tracked Venus

Maya astronomers recorded the movements of Venus with an accuracy that took European astronomy centuries to match. The tables survive in the Dresden Codex.

The record

The Dresden Codex, one of four surviving Maya books, contains a Venus table: a series of pages giving the positions of Venus over a cycle of 584 days, with corrections over longer periods. The Maya tracked Venus's appearances as morning and evening star and its periods of invisibility.

Maps, diagrams and images

The accuracy

The table's average value for the Venus cycle is very close to the modern figure of 583.92 days, and the corrections built into it show that the astronomers were refining the value over centuries. The purpose is argued: divination, the timing of warfare and ritual, and the correlation of Venus with the maize cycle have all been proposed.

The astronomy is documented; the use is interpretation.

What is established

Documented: the table and its values.

Documented: the corrections over long periods.

Documented: the codex itself and its date.

Contested: the purpose.

The label: documented.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the strongest evidence that Mesoamerican astronomy was quantitative, and because it undercuts the 'ancient people as passive observers' framing on which the star-map claims depend.
