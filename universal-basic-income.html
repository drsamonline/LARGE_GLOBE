Universal Basic Income

The strongest argument for a basic income is not that it makes people work more. It is that a welfare state built on conditions and paperwork produces the poverty it claims to fix.

The three claims

The freedom claim: unconditional cash removes the paternalism and the administrative machinery of means-tested benefits, and leaves people better able to take risks, care for family and refuse bad jobs.

Maps, diagrams and images

The work-disincentive claim: the standard objection is that a guaranteed income reduces labour supply, possibly by a lot, especially at the bottom of the wage distribution.

The adequacy claim: the version most often proposed — a modest payment funded by rearranging existing benefits — may leave some of the poorest worse off, which is why the details matter more than the principle.

What the experiments found

Finland's 2017–18 experiment paid 2,000 unemployed people €560 a month with no conditions. Employment did not improve much over two years, but recipients reported better wellbeing, less stress and more trust; the result was widely misreported as both a success and a failure depending on which outcome the headline wanted.

A large multi-site study in Kenya by GiveDirectly found that lump-sum transfers increased enterprise investment and did not increase spending on alcohol or tobacco, and that the local economy grew rather than being inflated away.

Stockton, California's guaranteed-income demonstration found full-time employment rose among recipients, with the biggest gains among people who had been given a stable floor while looking for work. A Denver study reported similar directions with smaller samples.

The honest summary: the predicted collapse in work has not appeared in these trials, and neither has a transformation of the labour market. Effects are real but modest, and the trials ran for one to three years, not a lifetime.

What no trial has tested

Universality. Almost every experiment pays a small group while everyone else keeps the existing system. That is a test of cash, not of a universal basic income, which would replace conditional benefits and change the whole wage bargain.

Funding. A payment large enough to live on costs several per cent of GDP. Whether it is financed by income tax, VAT, land value or by abolishing existing programmes decides who wins and who loses, and no experiment can answer that.

General equilibrium. If everyone received it, wages, rents and prices would adjust. Small trials deliberately avoid that, which is also why their results understate both the benefits and the costs.

The archive labels this live debate because the small-scale evidence is genuinely encouraging and the large-scale question is genuinely open.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is a policy idea that attracts both utopian overclaiming and reflexive dismissal, and because the actual trials are more interesting and more equivocal than either.
