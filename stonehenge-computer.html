Stonehenge as a Computer

In 1963 an astronomer argued that Stonehenge was a machine for predicting eclipses. The alignments are real; the machine is the part that did not survive scrutiny.

The claim

Gerald Hawkins's Stonehenge Decoded (1963) treated the monument as an observatory and a computer: he identified 165 key points, found that lines between them aligned with solar, lunar and stellar events, and argued that the resulting system could predict eclipses. Fred Hoyle endorsed a version of the argument, which gave it credibility.

Maps, diagrams and images

The objections

The core problem is degrees of freedom. With 165 points, there are thousands of possible lines, and some of them will align with something by chance. The test is whether the alignments are more frequent than a random arrangement of stones would produce, and the statistical work since has not supported the eclipse-predictor claim.

What has survived is narrower and solid: the monument is aligned on the solstices, and the sarsen circle and trilithons encode that alignment deliberately. That is an astronomical function. It is not a computer.

Where it stands

Documented: the solstice alignment, and its deliberate construction.

Documented: the lunar and solar associations in the wider monument.

Contested: whether more elaborate alignments were intended.

Not supported: eclipse prediction, which the statistics do not bear out.

The honest label: live debate about intent, with the 'computer' reading on the losing side.

Why it is in the archive

Because it shows how a real alignment becomes a machine in the telling, and because the distinction — marking a solstice is not computing an eclipse — is the same one the archive draws on every page.
