Scarcity or Ingenuity

The bet was $1,000 on five metals over ten years. Simon won, Ehrlich paid, and the argument has continued as if the bet had not happened.

The pessimistic tradition

Malthus argued that population grows geometrically while food grows arithmetically, so growth must end in famine. He was wrong about the century he lived in, for reasons he could not have anticipated — fossil fuels, synthetic fertiliser, mechanisation.

Maps, diagrams and images

The Limits to Growth, published by the Club of Rome in 1972, modelled a finite planet and predicted overshoot and collapse in the twenty-first century. Its business-as-usual scenario has not matched the data so far, but the model's central point — that exponential growth on a finite planet is temporary — is arithmetically true.

The modern degrowth position accepts the arithmetic and argues that rich countries should reduce throughput deliberately, rather than waiting for physical limits to impose it.

The ingenuity tradition

Julian Simon argued that resources are not fixed stocks but functions of human knowledge, and that scarcity shows up as price, which triggers substitution. The famous 1980 wager with Paul Ehrlich over five metals was won by Simon: their real prices fell over the decade.

The long-run data are broadly with him. Real prices of most minerals and of agricultural staples have fallen over two centuries despite enormous increases in consumption, because extraction, refining and substitution improved faster than depletion raised costs.

The counter-argument is not that prices will never rise but that the wager was a bad test: ten years, five metals, one decade of weak demand. A resource can be depleted slowly enough that prices fall for a century and then rise sharply — fisheries and groundwater are the standard examples, where the price signal arrives decades after the collapse.

What the evidence supports

Supported: for most minerals, the price-and-substitution mechanism has defeated simple depletion forecasts, and 'we are running out of X' has a poor record when X is a metal.

Not supported: that the mechanism is universal. It fails for resources that are not priced (the atmosphere, biodiversity, deep-sea ecosystems) and for ones where substitution takes longer than collapse.

The real argument: not whether growth is physically possible but how long substitution can run ahead of depletion and waste — and who bears the cost when it does not.

The archive labels this live debate because the historical record genuinely favours ingenuity and the extrapolation genuinely is a bet.

Why it is in the archive

Because both sides quote the same two centuries of data and draw opposite conclusions from it, and because the argument decides real policy on energy, minerals and climate.
