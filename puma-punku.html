Puma Punku Laser Cuts

Stone blocks at Tiwanaku have precise right angles and interlocking shapes, said to require lasers. They were cut with stone tools, and the site has been heavily disturbed.

The site

Puma Punku is part of the Tiwanaku complex in Bolivia, built around 500–1000 AD. It contains large blocks of red sandstone and andesite, some with complex interlocking shapes and precise right angles, scattered across the site.

Maps, diagrams and images

The claim

The claim is that the blocks were cut with a precision and regularity that requires modern machinery — lasers, or diamond tools — and that they are too hard for stone tools. The archaeological evidence contradicts this: the blocks are of moderate hardness, the surfaces show tool marks, and the shapes are consistent with known Andean stone-working techniques using harder stones, abrasives and patient labour.

The site has also been damaged: it was quarried for building material in the colonial period and later, and many blocks were moved or broken, which makes the 'impossible precision' claims harder to assess from what remains.

What is established

Documented: the blocks, their material, and their dates.

Documented: Andean stone-working techniques capable of the shapes.

Documented: the site's later disturbance.

Not established: any powered cutting.

The honest label: debunked.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the strongest-looking 'impossible precision' case, and because the answer is craft plus time, which is less exciting than a laser.
