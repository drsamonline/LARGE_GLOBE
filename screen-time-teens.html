Screen Time and Teen Mental Health

Two camps, one dataset, opposite headlines. The honest summary is that screen use correlates weakly with well-being, that the correlation is smaller than nearly everything else in the same data, and that causality is unresolved.

The case that something changed

Around 2012, rates of reported depression, self-harm and suicide-related hospitalisation among adolescents in several English-speaking countries began rising, especially among girls. Jonathan Haidt and Jean Twenge argue the timing matches smartphone and social-media adoption, and that the effect is large enough to matter at population scale.

The argument is partly about aggregates rather than individuals: even a small per-person effect, multiplied by near-universal exposure, could move population statistics.

The case against the strong claim

Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski's 2019 analysis of three large datasets found the association between digital technology use and adolescent well-being was negative but tiny — comparable to eating potatoes or wearing glasses, and far smaller than sleep, bullying or family relationships.

Measurement is a serious problem: screen time is self-reported, lumped into one number that ignores what the screen is being used for. A video call with a grandparent and an algorithmically boosted comparison feed are the same 'hours' in most datasets.

The trend data are also disputed: some countries show rises without matching smartphone adoption, and reported distress measures changed over the same period.

Where the disagreement actually is

Agreed: heavy use is associated with somewhat worse outcomes on average, and some specific patterns — late-night use, passive scrolling, appearance comparison — look worse than others.

Disputed: whether the association is causal, and how large the causal effect is. Randomised and quasi-experimental studies exist but are small and short.

Overstated on both sides: 'phones are destroying a generation' and 'the effect is trivial, nothing to see'. The second can be true statistically and still miss a real, unevenly distributed harm.

The archive labels this live debate because the disagreement is about inference from weak observational data, not about whether teenagers use phones.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is a case where the moral panic framing and the debunking framing are both partly right, and the archive's job is to keep the numbers visible.
