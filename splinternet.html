The Splinternet

The internet is fragmenting into national and regional blocs, with different rules, different infrastructure and different content. It is measurable, and the measurement is the interesting part.

The claim

The splinternet is the argument that the global internet is breaking into separate blocs — China's, Russia's, the EU's regulatory sphere, and the US platform sphere — with different content, rules and infrastructure. The term gained currency in the 2010s as data-localisation laws spread.

Maps, diagrams and images

The evidence

The evidence is measurable in several ways: border gateway protocol anomalies and route filtering, content availability by country, the growth of national filtering systems, data-localisation statutes, and the physical routing of traffic through national exchanges. Measurements find increasing fragmentation on some axes and stubborn globalisation on others — a single undersea cable still carries traffic between blocs.

The honest version is that fragmentation is real and uneven: states have gained control over the edges, while the core protocols and the physical layer remain largely shared.

What is established

Documented: national filtering, data-localisation laws, and route-level filtering.

Documented: the physical layer remains largely shared.

Measured: fragmentation on content and routing axes, unevenly.

Contested: how far it will go, and whether 'splinternet' overstates a partial process.

The honest label: documented — a real and measurable trend with an arguable name.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the archive's reminder that infrastructure claims can be checked — and because the metaphor is doing more work than the measurement in most of the writing about it.
