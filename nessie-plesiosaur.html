Nessie as a Plesiosaur

The Loch Ness Monster is usually drawn as a plesiosaur. That identification has a specific problem: a breeding population of large air-breathing reptiles in a cold lake would leave traces, and none has been found.

The sightings

Reports of a creature in Loch Ness became a public phenomenon in 1933, after a new road opened along the shore. The most famous image, the 'Surgeon's Photograph' of 1934, was admitted as a hoax in 1994 by one of the men involved.

Maps, diagrams and images

The plesiosaur problem

A plesiosaur is a marine reptile that breathed air and gave birth to live young. A surviving population in a freshwater lake would need to be large enough to breed — dozens of individuals — and would surface to breathe regularly, which ninety years of observation has not recorded.

Surveys have included sonar, submersibles, and environmental DNA sampling in 2018, which recovered DNA from fish, birds, plants and mammals but no reptile. The lake's biomass is also too small to support a population of large predators.

Where it stands

Documented: the sightings, the hoax, and the surveys.

Not found: any reptile DNA, carcass, or verified photograph.

Not plausible: a breeding population of large reptiles in the lake.

Not excluded: large fish, seals, or misidentification.

The honest label: fringe — a claim tested by every available method, with no positive result.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the most thoroughly investigated cryptid, and the investigation is the interesting part: the more methods were applied, the less there was.
