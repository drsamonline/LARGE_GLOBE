MSG Syndrome

A letter to a medical journal created a condition. Blinded trials did not find it.

The claim

A 1968 letter to the New England Journal of Medicine described numbness and palpitations after Chinese meals, attributed to monosodium glutamate. The idea became 'Chinese restaurant syndrome' and drove decades of consumer avoidance.

Maps, diagrams and images

The evidence

Double-blind trials found that most people reporting the syndrome did not react to MSG when they could not tell what they were consuming, and regulatory reviews have found it safe at ordinary levels. Some people report symptoms at large doses taken without food, which is documented.

What is established

Documented: the letter and the panic.

Documented: the blinded trials.

Not supported: a general syndrome.

The label: debunked.

Why it is in the archive

Because a single letter in a journal can shape a food culture for fifty years.
