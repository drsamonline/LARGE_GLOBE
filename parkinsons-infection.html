The Infection Hypothesis of Parkinson's

The claim that Parkinson's begins with an infection or an environmental trigger that travels to the brain. The pathology spreads in a pattern that fits, and the trigger has not been found.

The observation

Parkinson's disease involves the accumulation of misfolded alpha-synuclein protein, and the pathology spreads through the brain in a characteristic sequence described by Braak and colleagues. The pattern suggests a spreading process, and the vagus nerve and the gut have been proposed as entry routes.

Maps, diagrams and images

The evidence

Supporting evidence includes the finding that truncal vagotomy — cutting the vagus — was associated with a lower risk of Parkinson's in a large cohort, and animal work showing that alpha-synuclein can spread along neural pathways. Candidate triggers include infections, pesticides and other environmental exposures.

No specific infectious agent has been identified, and the hypothesis is a framework rather than a finding.

Where it stands

Documented: the spreading pathology.

Documented: the vagotomy association.

Documented: experimental spread along neurons.

Not identified: a specific trigger.

The honest label: live debate.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is a strong hypothesis with good circumstantial evidence and no identified cause, which is the normal state of neurodegenerative research.
