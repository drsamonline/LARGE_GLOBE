Predictive Coding

The claim that the brain is a prediction machine, constantly modelling the world and correcting the errors. It explains a lot and has been hard to prove.

The theory

Predictive coding proposes that the brain generates predictions about sensory input and transmits only the errors — the differences between prediction and input — up the hierarchy, with predictions flowing down. It is closely related to the free energy principle and to Bayesian accounts of perception.

Maps, diagrams and images

The evidence

Support comes from visual neuroscience: neural responses to predictable stimuli are suppressed, and error signals have been identified in specific cortical layers. Predictive coding explains a wide range of phenomena, including illusions and the effects of expectation on perception.

The criticisms are that the framework is hard to falsify in its general form, that the evidence for layer-specific predictions is contested, and that many findings fit other models equally well.

Where it stands

Documented: the theory and its influence.

Supported: suppression of predictable input.

Contested: the laminar evidence.

Contested: falsifiability.

The honest label: live debate.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the most influential framework in cognitive neuroscience and the hardest to test, which is the same problem the free energy principle has.
