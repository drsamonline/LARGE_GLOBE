Secret Societies

The Illuminati existed for about ten years, was banned, had its papers seized and published — and has been running the world ever since, according to people who have never read the papers.

This dossier is unusual because the secret society at the centre of the modern myth is fully documented. We know who founded it, when, why, how many members it had, how it was organised, how it was suppressed, and what happened to its archive. Almost none of that survives in the popular version.

The Bavarian Illuminati, 1776–1785

Adam Weishaupt was a 28-year-old professor of canon law at the University of Ingolstadt, a former Jesuit, and a partisan of the radical Enlightenment. On 1 May 1776 he founded a small society with a handful of senior students. They called themselves Perfectibilists; the name Illuminati came later.

The goal, as the University of Munich’s own history puts it, was to build an elite cadre that would eventually occupy the key positions of the state and subvert the authority of the territorial princes — not through revolution, but through education and placement. Britannica summarises the programme as replacing established religion with a cult of reason and overthrowing monarchical government. Members included the astronomer Johann Bode, the writer Friedrich Nicolai, and the philosopher Friedrich Jacobi. At its height the order had roughly 1,400 members — a serious network, and a small one.

It was organised on Jesuit lines, with grades, ciphers and code names; Weishaupt was “Spartacus”. It recruited inside Masonic lodges, which is the origin of the idea that the two are the same organisation.

The suppression

Growth attracted attention. On 22 June 1784 Elector Karl Theodor issued a general edict against unauthorised secret societies; on 2 March 1785 a second edict named the Illuminati and Freemasonry specifically. Weishaupt lost his professorship and fled. Houses were searched, and the order’s papers — internal correspondence, statutes, grade texts, membership lists — were seized and then published.

By 1786 the order was effectively finished. The Bavarian Academy of Sciences’ own history states that it “expired after barely ten years of existence and did not revive during the French Revolution.”

That publication is the twist. The Illuminati were destroyed by transparency, in the 1780s, and the documents have been available to historians ever since. The University of Erfurt now runs a dedicated Illuminati research unit, working through the seized archive.

Where the world-domination story came from

It came from the losers. Ex-members denounced the order, and a French émigré priest, Augustin Barruel, published a conspiracy thesis arguing that the French Revolution had been engineered by Freemasons and Illuminati. The claim was politically useful — it made a systemic upheaval into a plot — and it never needed evidence, because the order’s real papers could be reinterpreted rather than read.

Every later version is a descendant of that move, including the twentieth-century paperbacks and the twenty-first-century videos. The structure is: a real, small, extinct organisation + a real archive nobody checks + an unlimited scope.

Freemasonry, which was never secret

Freemasonry is a fraternal organisation with rituals, not a conspiracy. Its documented history:

  • 1390 — the Regius Poem, the earliest known Masonic manuscript, describing operative stonemasons’ craft rules.
  • 1717 — four London lodges merge to form the first Grand Lodge, the conventional founding of modern speculative Freemasonry.
  • 1738 — the Catholic Church condemns the movement; roughly twenty papal decrees follow over the centuries.
  • Since then — lodges are listed in public directories, halls are on maps, and members appear in every walk of public life, which is the actual reason for the “they’re everywhere” claim.

Masonry is not a religion, though members are asked to believe in a Supreme Being. Its secrecy is ceremonial — passwords and rituals — not informational. A lodge’s existence, address and often its officers are public.

The Eye of Providence

The eye-in-a-triangle on the US one-dollar bill is the most-shared “Illuminati” image in the world. It is not Masonic. It is part of the Great Seal of the United States, designed in 1782 by a committee including Benjamin Franklin, Thomas Jefferson and John Adams, and it represents divine providence — a standard Christian emblem of the period. The motto below, annuit coeptis, and the pyramid’s novus ordo seclorum, are Latin tags from Virgil. The seal was adopted nine years before the Illuminati were suppressed and on a different continent.

The test that matters

A secret society that runs the world needs three things: continuity, coordination and capacity. The Illuminati have none. They existed for under a decade, were destroyed by a police action, and their entire internal archive is in a university library being studied by historians who publish what they find.

Meanwhile, the organisations that really do coordinate quietly — corporate boards, party caucuses, defence contractors, intelligence alliances — operate in the open, with names, addresses and annual reports. That is the actual scandal, and it is duller and more consequential than the myth.

The one that is simply true: Bohemian Grove

Not every “secret society” story is a conspiracy. Bohemian Grove is a real, documented institution: a 2,700-acre private camp in Monte Rio, Sonoma County, California, owned by the Bohemian Club of San Francisco, which was founded in 1872. Every July it hosts a two-week gathering of members and guests — historically including US presidents, cabinet members, Fortune 500 executives and artists.

The camp has a lake, a stage, a 40-foot owl statue (the “Owl Shrine”), and a theatrical opening ceremony called the Cremation of Care. Presidents from both parties have attended. In 2000 the journalist Alex Jones filmed inside without authorisation, and the footage — of the Cremation of Care in particular — became the visual vocabulary for a decade of conspiracy content.

The distinction that matters: the existence of an elite, men-only, off-the-record gathering is not a conspiracy theory. It is a fact, and an uncomfortable one for anyone who thinks influence is evenly distributed. What is not documented is the part the internet adds — that decisions are made there, that it is a shadow government, that the ceremony is anything other than theatre.

The archive treats this as the useful control case. Bohemian Grove shows what a real secret looks like: a private club that keeps the press out, whose members deny nothing because nothing needs denying. The Tartaria story, by contrast, needs the evidence destroyed to survive. Both are about power; only one has photographs, deeds and a tax record.
