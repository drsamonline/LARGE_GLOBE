The Streisand Effect

An attempt to suppress information draws attention to it. The name comes from a photograph of a house, and the mechanism is now a standard part of online strategy.

The episode

In 2003 the photographer Kenneth Adelman published aerial photographs of the California coast as part of a coastal survey, including one of Barbra Streisand's house. Her lawyers demanded its removal; the site reported 420,000 visitors in the following month, and the image was widely reproduced. The term was coined by Mike Masnick shortly afterwards.

The mechanism

The effect is not magic. A takedown attempt supplies three things: a signal that the material matters, a story for journalists, and a reason for people to mirror the content. The result is the opposite of the intent, and it is now well enough understood that organisations plan for it.

It is a documented case rather than a universal law: many takedowns succeed quietly, and the effect requires an audience that notices the attempt.

What is established

Documented: the original episode, with traffic figures.

Documented: many subsequent cases.

The mechanism: attention drawn by suppression.

Not universal: most takedowns do not backfire.

The label: documented.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the clearest case of a strategy that reliably produces the outcome it was meant to prevent.
