Assembly Theory

A proposed measure of how complex an object is, by counting the minimum steps needed to build it. Its authors argue it explains selection and can detect life; its critics argue it measures construction, not causation.

The proposal

Assembly theory, developed by Lee Cronin and Sara Walker from 2017, assigns an 'assembly index' to a molecule: the minimum number of joining operations needed to build it from basic units. The claim is that objects with high assembly index must have been produced by a process of selection, because random assembly is astronomically unlikely, and that the index therefore gives an empirical signature of life and of evolution.

The 2023 paper in Nature reported measuring assembly indices by mass spectrometry and argued that the approach unifies selection, information and causation.

The objections

The criticisms are specific. The index measures the shortest construction path, which is a property of the object and of the chemist's choice of building blocks, not of its history. A crystal or a polymer can have a high index without selection. And the mass-spectrometry measurement of the index has been challenged as not actually recovering the quantity it claims.

The theory's defenders reply that the index applies to ensembles rather than single molecules, and that the objections miss the statistical framing. The exchange is ongoing and technical.

Where it stands

Documented: the index, its definition, and the published measurements.

Contested: whether the index tracks selection rather than construction.

Contested: whether the experimental method measures what it claims.

Not established: a general account of causation or of the origin of life.

The honest label: live debate — a serious proposal in an early, argumentative phase.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the archive's most current page: a live scientific argument from the last three years, filed with the same caution as the historical ones, and because it is a good test of whether the archive can cover a debate before it is settled.
