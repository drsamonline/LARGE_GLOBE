The Baader–Meinhof Effect

Learn a new word and it appears everywhere for a week. Nothing has changed except your attention — which is why it is also called the frequency illusion.

The effect

The Baader–Meinhof phenomenon is the experience of encountering a recently learned thing everywhere. The name comes from a 1994 letter to the St. Paul Pioneer Press, in which a reader described learning about the German faction and then seeing it mentioned repeatedly.

Maps, diagrams and images

The mechanism has two parts, and neither is mysterious. Selective attention makes you notice the thing once it is salient, and confirmation bias makes the notices memorable while the non-notices are not counted. The linguist Arnold Zwicky named it the 'frequency illusion' in 2005, which is the better name.

Why it matters here

Because it is the mechanism behind a large fraction of the claims in this archive: a pattern noticed after it is named, and then reported as though the naming revealed it. It is also why 'I keep seeing this everywhere' is not evidence of anything except attention.

What is established

Documented: the effect is robust and easily demonstrated.

Documented: it is produced by selective attention plus confirmation bias.

Documented: the frequency of the thing does not change.

Not established: any external cause. There is nothing to explain beyond the two biases.

The useful lesson: salience is not prevalence, and the archive's job is mostly to keep them apart.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the perceptual error underneath several of the stranger pages here, and because naming it is the cheapest debunking tool the archive has.
