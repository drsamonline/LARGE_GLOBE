Lead and Crime

Violent crime fell through the 1990s in every rich country at once, and nobody predicted it. One explanation is that a generation stopped breathing leaded petrol as children.

The claim

Children exposed to lead — from petrol additive, paint, water pipes and industrial dust — show measurable effects on impulse control, attention and aggression. If a whole generation was exposed and then a whole generation was not, violent crime should rise and then fall about twenty years later, when each cohort reaches its peak offending age.

The hypothesis was pushed hardest by Rick Nevin, an economist, and by Jessica Wolpaw Reyes, whose 2007 paper in Environmental Research found that childhood lead exposure explained a substantial share of the variation in violent crime across US states, with the expected lag.

The timing is striking. Lead was removed from American petrol between 1975 and 1986; violent crime peaked around 1991–1993 and fell steeply for two decades. The same pattern appears, with different timings, in many other countries.

Where the science is strong

  • Individual-level effects are well established. Cohort studies link childhood lead exposure to lower IQ, attention problems, and behavioural measures associated with impulsivity. The dose–response relationship is consistent.
  • Natural experiments support it. The Flint water crisis produced documented rises in children's blood lead and measurable developmental effects. Communities with abrupt exposure changes show corresponding changes.
  • Mechanism is plausible. Lead disrupts neurotransmitter systems and brain development in ways that could plausibly affect self-regulation.
Where it is contested

Ecological fallacy. Population-level correlation between lead and crime does not prove that the individuals committing crimes were the exposed ones.

Confounding. The same decades saw abortion legalisation, policing changes, demographic shifts, crack's rise and fall, and economic restructuring. A single variable explaining a large share of a complex trend is a strong claim.

Cross-national inconsistency. Some countries with different lead timelines do not fit the model neatly, and replication has been mixed.

The effect size is disputed. Estimates range from "a major driver" to "one contributor among many". The literature has not converged.

The honest verdict

This is genuinely a live scientific debate, not a conspiracy and not settled. The strongest defensible statement is that lead exposure is a real cause of neurological and behavioural harm, that removing it was one of the great public-health wins of the century, and that it plausibly contributed to the crime decline — with the size of that contribution still argued.

What makes it valuable to this archive is the contrast with documented corporate concealment: the lead industry's own history of suppressing evidence is well documented, and the harm is not in doubt. The uncertainty is about the crime statistics, not about whether lead was dangerous.
