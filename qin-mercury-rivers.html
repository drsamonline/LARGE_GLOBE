Qin's Mercury Rivers

The First Emperor's tomb is said to contain rivers of mercury under a ceiling of pearls. The soil around the mound shows elevated mercury, and the tomb has never been opened.

The record

The historian Sima Qian, writing about a century after the emperor's death, describes the mausoleum of Qin Shi Huang as containing palaces, traps, and rivers and seas of mercury made to flow mechanically, beneath a ceiling set with pearls. The mound has never been excavated.

Maps, diagrams and images

The evidence

Soil samples from around the mound have shown elevated mercury concentrations, which is consistent with the account but not proof of it. The tomb itself is sealed, and Chinese policy is not to excavate it — partly because the technology to preserve what is inside does not exist, and partly because of the reported mercury hazard.

The Terracotta Army pits nearby are excavated, and they are a fraction of the complex.

Where it stands

Documented: the account, the mound and the surrounding complex.

Documented: elevated mercury in the soil.

Not established: the rivers, the pearls or the traps.

Not excavated: the tomb chamber.

The honest label: live debate.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is a claim from a text that the soil partly supports, and because the reason it is untested is a policy decision, not a lack of interest.
