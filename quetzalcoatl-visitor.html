Quetzalcoatl the Visitor

A feathered serpent god was recast in the twentieth century as a pale-skinned teacher from overseas. The evidence for the visitor is a sixteenth-century text read backwards.

The god

Quetzalcoatl is a Mesoamerican deity — feathered serpent, wind, learning, and in some traditions a legendary ruler of Tollan. The iconography is ancient and widespread, from Teotihuacan to the Aztec capital.

Maps, diagrams and images

The visitor

The claim that Quetzalcoatl was a bearded, pale-skinned foreigner who came to teach civilisation rests on sixteenth-century Spanish accounts written after the conquest, some of them by friars interpreting local tradition through their own expectations. The 'return of Quetzalcoatl' story, used to explain Moctezuma's response to Cortés, is now widely regarded by historians as at least partly a post-conquest construction.

Mesoamerican depictions of the god do not show a bearded European, and the traditions describe a deity and a legendary king, not a visitor from elsewhere.

What is established

Documented: the deity, its iconography, and its antiquity.

Documented: the sixteenth-century sources and their context.

Contested: how much of the 'return' story is pre-conquest.

Not established: any foreign teacher, or any visitor.

The honest label: debunked.

Why it is in the archive

Because it shows how a colonial-era reading became an ancient-astronaut staple, and because the correction comes from the same sources the claim uses.
