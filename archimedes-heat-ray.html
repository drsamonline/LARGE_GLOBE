Archimedes' Heat Ray

Mirrors on the walls of Syracuse, focusing sunlight onto Roman ships. The story is late, the physics is marginal, and the experiments have produced smoke and no fire.

The story

During the Roman siege of Syracuse in 212 BC, Archimedes is said to have used mirrors to focus sunlight on the Roman ships and set them alight. The story appears in late sources — the earliest detailed accounts are centuries after the event — and was repeated by medieval and Renaissance authors.

The tests

The experiments have been done repeatedly. In 1973 a Greek engineer, Ioannis Sakkas, set fire to a tarred wooden boat at about 50 metres using 70 mirrors, in calm conditions. In 2005 a team at MIT tested the idea with modern materials and concluded that a single mirror array could not ignite a ship at any plausible siege distance within a useful time. Television experiments have produced smoke at short range on stationary targets.

The conclusion is that the effect is possible under ideal conditions, and that a siege is not ideal conditions: ships move, the sun moves, the mirrors would need continuous aim, and the target would need to be dry and stationary.

Where it stands

Documented: Archimedes, the siege, and the late literary tradition of the mirrors.

Demonstrated: ignition at short range with many mirrors on a stationary target.

Not demonstrated: ignition at siege range under battle conditions.

Not established: that it happened. No contemporary source describes it.

The honest label: live debate about a physical possibility, with the historical evidence thin.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is a case where the physics is real, the engineering is marginal, and the history is later than the story — three separate weaknesses that are usually collapsed into one claim.
