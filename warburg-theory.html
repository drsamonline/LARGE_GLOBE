The Warburg Effect

Cancer cells ferment glucose even when oxygen is available. Whether that is a cause of cancer or a consequence is the argument, and the difference matters for treatment.

The observation

Otto Warburg observed in 1924 that cancer cells ferment glucose to lactate even in the presence of oxygen — aerobic glycolysis — rather than using oxidative phosphorylation. The phenomenon is documented across many tumour types and is the basis of PET imaging, which detects the increased glucose uptake.

Maps, diagrams and images

The interpretation

Warburg argued that the metabolic change was the cause of cancer, a claim he maintained against the emerging genetic account. The modern view is that the metabolic shift is usually a consequence of oncogenic mutations and the tumour environment, though it may contribute to progression.

The clinical interest is in targeting the metabolism, with mixed results so far.

Where it stands

Documented: aerobic glycolysis in tumours.

Documented: its use in PET imaging.

Contested: whether it is a cause.

Favoured: a consequence, with contributions to progression.

The honest label: live debate.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is a real metabolic signature with a large causal claim attached, and because the imaging makes the effect visible in every hospital.
