How to use this

Reading a dossier

Every page in the archive has the same shape, so you always know where you are in it. This page is the map of that shape, and of the three ways in.

OneStart with the label, not the title

Under the headline is a coloured chip. It says how strong the evidence is, on a four-part scale used across all 1010 dossiers:

Documented
Primary documents, peer review or official records. The CIA really did run MKUltra; plate tectonics really did replace continental drift.
Live debate
Argued in the literature, usually at the margins. The claim has serious proponents and serious critics, and the argument is about evidence rather than about motives.
Fringe claim
Widely circulated, no accepted evidence, no working mechanism. Not the same as a lie, and not the same as undecided.
Debunked
Tested and falsified, or admitted fabricated. Something specific went wrong, and the page says what.

Some pages add a gloss of their own — Ended by DNA, A thermal blanket, Geology, not architecture. The colour is always the canonical label; the words are that page's summary of it. If a gloss ever contradicts its colour, that is a bug worth reporting.

TwoThe five parts of a page

The standfirst is the whole argument in three sentences, written so that reading only it is not misleading.

At a glance, in the right-hand panel, is the status in six facts: who proposed it, when, the strongest objection, and what would settle it. On a phone it sits below the text.

The body runs in a fixed order — the claim as its best proponents state it, their strongest evidence, the response, and what the response does not answer. A claim is never answered in a form its holders would not recognise.

The sources are at the foot, with a link each. The linked source outranks the page: if the paper and this page disagree, the paper is right and the page is a bug.

The citation block gives the page in APA, MLA, Chicago and BibTeX, so it can be footnoted without retyping the title.

ThreeThree ways in

If you want a route, start with the reading guide. It sequences a dozen dossiers so that each one teaches the reading skill the next one needs.

If you want a shelf, use themes. Every dossier belongs to exactly one of seven, and neighbouring claims share the same kind of evidence.

If you want a thing you half remember, press ⌘K or Ctrl-K anywhere, or open search. Both look at all 992 records: 1010 dossiers and the 830 rows of the five collections.

FourWhen you disagree

Two rules make that useful. First, check the source before concluding the page is wrong: the link is there so you can. Second, if the page is wrong, say which kind of wrong it is — misattributed, wrongly labelled, dead link, wrong number, wrong credit. The corrections page explains what happens next, and what does not: nothing is deleted for being embarrassing.

Where a page states a probability, a date or a figure, the number is either measured on the page or attributed to whoever measured it. Where it is neither, that is a bug.

FiveWhat the archive will not do

It will not tell you what to conclude. It tells you what was claimed, by whom, in what year, and what the measurement said. That is a deliberate limit, and it is the reason the labels are about evidence rather than about truth.
