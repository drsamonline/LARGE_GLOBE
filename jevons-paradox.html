The Jevons Paradox

In 1865 William Stanley Jevons observed that more efficient steam engines increased Britain's coal consumption, not reduced it. The pattern has been embarrassing energy policy ever since.

The observation

Jevons argued that improving the efficiency of coal use would make coal-powered processes cheaper, expand their use, and raise total consumption. He was writing about a genuine historical episode, and the logic is straightforward: efficiency lowers the effective price of a service.

The same pattern appears in lighting, refrigeration, vehicle fuel economy and computing. Data centres are the current test case: each computation has become cheaper, and total electricity demand has risen.

Direct and indirect rebound

Economists distinguish a direct rebound — you drive more because driving is cheaper — from indirect effects, where money saved is spent on other energy-consuming goods, and from economy-wide effects, where productivity gains raise growth.

Estimates of direct rebound for household energy are typically in the range of 5–30 per cent; economy-wide rebound is much harder to estimate and much more contested, with some studies suggesting it can exceed 100 per cent for particular technologies.

What the evidence supports

Supported: efficiency improvements do not reliably reduce total consumption by the amount a naive calculation suggests. Rebound is real and routinely omitted from policy projections.

Not supported: the strong claim that efficiency always increases consumption, so efficiency policy is pointless. That depends on the technology, the market and the time horizon.

Policy consequence: pair efficiency standards with price or cap mechanisms if the goal is absolute reduction. Efficiency alone is a weak instrument for that.

The archive labels this live debate because the direction is accepted and the magnitude — which decides the policy — is not.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is a rare case where a counter-intuitive economic idea is genuinely well founded, and where both boosters and critics of efficiency policy quote it selectively.
