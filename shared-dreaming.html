Shared Dreaming

Two people report the same dream on the same night. It is one of the most compelling experiences a person can have and one of the hardest claims to test, because dreams are private and reports are not.

The claim

Shared dreaming is the claim that two or more people can experience the same dream — meeting in it, or reporting the same content from the same night. It appears in the dream-telepathy literature of the 1960s, in the lucid-dreaming community, and in a large popular literature.

The evidence

The classic experiments were run at the Maimonides Medical Center in the 1960s and 1970s: a sender looked at a target image while a sleeper in another room dreamed, and judges compared dream reports with targets. The results were reported as positive and have not survived replication; the methodology has been criticised for judge selection and analysis flexibility.

Modern attempts in lucid-dreaming communities are anecdotal. The structural problem is that dream content is only known from reports given after waking, subject to reconstruction, expectation and the desire to have shared something.

What is established

Documented: people sometimes report similar dreams, and the experience is often meaningful to them.

Documented: the historical experiments, and their failure to replicate.

Not established: any transfer of dream content between people.

The methodological obstacle: reports are retrospective, and both parties know the hypothesis.

The honest label: fringe. A testable claim in principle, with no positive evidence that survives controls.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the archive's cleanest case of a claim that would be easy to test and has not been, and because the experience behind it — two people sure they met in a dream — is real even when the meeting is not.
