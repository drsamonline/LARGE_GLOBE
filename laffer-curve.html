The Laffer Curve

Tax revenue is zero at 0% and at 100%, so somewhere in between it peaks. The shape is argued.

The argument

The Laffer curve holds that tax revenue is zero at a zero rate and at a 100% rate, and rises to a maximum somewhere between. It is used to argue that cutting rates can raise revenue.

Maps, diagrams and images

The evidence

The endpoints are arithmetic; the position of the peak is empirical and disputed, and most estimates place it well above current rates in developed countries. Claims that specific cuts paid for themselves are documented and mostly not supported.

Where it stands

Documented: the endpoints.

Contested: the peak's position.

Mostly not supported: self-financing cuts.

The honest label: live debate.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is a curve with two certain points and one contested one, and it is quoted as if all three were known.

What the curve is and what it is not

The curve states that revenue is zero at a tax rate of zero and zero at a rate of a hundred per cent, so somewhere between there is a maximum. That much is arithmetic; where the peak sits is an empirical question, and it varies by tax and by time.

The political use of the curve assumes the current rate is above the peak, which is the part that requires evidence rather than a drawing. The archive keeps the page because a diagram that is true in form is routinely used to assert a magnitude it does not contain.
