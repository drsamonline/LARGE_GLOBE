Urban Legends & Creepypasta

A Photoshop contest produced a monster. Five years later, two twelve-year-olds tried to kill their friend for him. This is the only page in the archive where the fiction caused the harm.

“Creepypasta” is a portmanteau of “copy-paste”: short horror stories, usually written as fake first-person accounts, designed to be copied across forums intact. Folklorists treat them as a genuine contemporary form — the digital descendant of the campfire story — and they have produced at least one figure that escaped the internet entirely.

Slender Man: a documented birth

On 10 June 2009, a comedy forum called Something Awful ran a thread asking users to create paranormal images in Photoshop. A contributor posting as “Victor Surge” — the artist Eric Knudsen — uploaded two fake photographs purporting to be from the 1980s: children in a playground, and behind them a tall, faceless figure in a dark suit.

He attached fragments of text implying that fourteen children and a photographer had vanished. The BBC’s account is precise about the effect: the fragments, the missing details and the deliberate incompleteness are what made it spread. A tidier story would have died in the thread.

What followed is the best-documented case of collaborative folklore in the internet era. Thousands of people added drawings, videos and stories; the YouTube series Marble Hornets built a narrative around him; folklorist Jeff Tolbert has described the process as an “open-sourcing of storytelling”. The academic literature followed — a 2016 article in Children’s Literature and a 2018 university-press collection, Slender Man Is Coming, both treat creepypasta as digital legendry with the same rules as oral tradition: repetition with variation, claimed realism, and an unresolved frame.

The stabbing

On 31 May 2014, in Waukesha, Wisconsin, three twelve-year-old girls went into woods on a “birdwatching” trip. One was stabbed nineteen times and left for dead; she crawled to a road and survived. The other two were arrested and charged. They told investigators they had acted to prove themselves to Slender Man and to become his servants.

This is the case that forces a serious point. Fiction is not harmless because it is fiction, and the internet is not a separate world. But the causal story is not “the story made them do it” — it is that a shared fantasy, an untreated mental-health crisis, and two children with no adult between them and the idea produced a crime. Snopes and the BBC both cover the trial and the aftermath; the victim recovered and the attackers were committed to psychiatric care.

Momo: a hoax studied like a disease

Between 2018 and 2019, parents across several countries were warned that a “Momo challenge” was appearing inside children’s videos — a gaunt figure with black hair and a wide mouth, ordering children to self-harm.

It was not real. The image was a photograph of a sculpture by a Japanese artist, taken out of context; there was no evidence of any child being harmed; and the alarm was amplified overwhelmingly by adults, not children. A 2021 paper in the Journal for the Academic Study of Religion analyses it as “a major online demonic hoax” — a legend whose engine was adult anxiety about screens, not the screens themselves.

The pattern repeats: a striking image, a plausible threat to children, a warning shared by people who care, and a news cycle that cannot be un-shared. It is the same mechanism as the cryptid and mystery dossiers, with a moral panic attached.

Crop circles: the confession

In September 1991 two Englishmen, Doug Bower and Dave Chorley, told the press they had been making crop circles since the late 1970s using a plank, a rope and a wire sight. The New York Times reported it under the headline “2 ‘Jovial Con Men’ Demystify Those Crop Circles in Britain”.

The confession did not end crop circles — the form continued and grew more elaborate — but it converted the question from “what makes them?” to “who makes them, and why do we want them to be something else?” The image on this page is of a known fake, which is the honest illustration for the topic.

Why horror stories are the perfect conspiracy format

  • Unresolved by design. A good creepypasta leaves one detail missing. That gap is where belief enters.
  • First person, no author. The text claims to be testimony, and the author is a username.
  • Free to alter. Copy-paste means every retelling is also an edit, like oral tradition.
  • Unfalsifiable by construction. “The footage was destroyed” is a plot point, not an excuse.

Set those four next to the claims in the rest of this archive. The hollow Earth, the ice wall and the Tartarian empire all share the same four properties — which is precisely why they are so easy to believe, and why this site insists on the boring work of finding sources.

Polybius: the arcade cabinet that never was

The story: in 1981 a black arcade cabinet appeared in Portland, Oregon arcades. The game — supposedly called Polybius, after the Greek historian — was a vector-graphics puzzle that caused amnesia, night terrors and hallucinations in players. Men in black suits were seen collecting data from the machines, and then the cabinets vanished.

It is one of the most durable pieces of internet folklore, and it has almost no documentary basis. There are no photographs of a cabinet, no arcade operator's log, no manufacturer records, no contemporary newspaper report, and no player who can be identified. The earliest known appearance of the story is a coinop.org entry written around 2000 — two decades after the alleged events.

That gap is the tell. A real cabinet in public arcades for weeks would have left a trace: a service manual, a tax record, a photograph in a local paper, an op's route sheet. The legend instead carries the fingerprints of its own era — the “men in black” motif from 1980s UFO lore, the memory-damage motif from later creepypasta, the sinister-German-name motif from a very specific strand of internet humour.

It is a great story, and it is almost certainly a story. See the deep web file for the same dynamics applied to a real-but-duller phenomenon.
