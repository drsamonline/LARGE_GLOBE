Polywater

A form of water said to be denser and more viscous, with extraordinary properties. It was contaminated ordinary water.

The claim

Soviet scientists reported in the 1960s a polymerised form of water with higher boiling point, density and viscosity, formed in narrow capillaries. The claim was taken seriously in the West, and research programmes followed.

Maps, diagrams and images

The exposure

Careful work showed the properties came from contamination — silicates and other impurities leached from the glass — and that the substance was not a new form of water. The episode is documented as a cautionary tale about instrumentation and contamination.

What is established

Documented: the reports.

Documented: the contamination.

Not real: polywater.

Explained: by impurities.

The label: debunked.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the best-known artefact in chemistry, and the mechanism is ordinary.

How a mistake travelled so far

In the 1960s Soviet scientists reported a form of water with anomalous properties — higher boiling point, higher viscosity, a different spectrum — produced by condensing vapour in narrow quartz capillaries. The claim reached the Western literature, was reproduced in several laboratories, and acquired a theoretical explanation involving polymerised water.

The anomaly was contamination. The capillaries leached silica and other impurities into the condensate, and the properties were those of a dilute silicate solution. The decisive work came from careful cleaning and from a calculation showing that a polymerised water structure could not be stable — which is why the episode is the standard example of a result that replicates and is still wrong.
