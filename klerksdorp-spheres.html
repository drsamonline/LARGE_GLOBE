The Klerksdorp Spheres

Grooved stone spheres from a South African mine, three billion years old, were said to be manufactured. They are concretions, and the grooves are where crystals grew.

The objects

The Klerksdorp spheres come from pyrophyllite deposits near Ottosdal in South Africa, in rock around three billion years old. They are small — a few centimetres across — and some have parallel grooves around the middle. Mineralogists have known them since the 1970s, and they are sold to tourists.

Maps, diagrams and images

The claim and the answer

The claim is that they are manufactured: too spherical, too regular, with grooves that look machined. The answer is that they are concretions — mineral masses that form by accretion around a nucleus in sedimentary rock — and that the grooves are where crystals of a harder mineral grew and later weathered out, or where the concretion grew in a layered environment.

The surfaces are not as regular as they look in photographs, the 'balance' claims do not survive measurement, and there is no evidence of tooling: no concentric lathe marks, no consistent dimensions.

What is established

Documented: the objects, their age, their locality and their mineralogy.

Documented: the concretion mechanism, and the crystal-groove explanation.

Not established: manufacture, or any precision beyond the natural range.

The error: judging regularity from photographs and anecdotes rather than measurement.

The honest label: debunked.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the archetype of the genre: an object that is genuinely odd, genuinely old, and genuinely natural, with a photograph that makes it look otherwise.
