Proven Conspiracies

Real conspiracies exist, and they are on the record. That is precisely why the unfalsifiable ones are recognisable: they look nothing like this.

“Conspiracy theory” has become an insult, but a conspiracy is simply an agreement to do something harmful in secret — a crime with more than one participant. Some of those were committed by governments, and were later proven with documents.

Maps, diagrams and images

Six cases follow. Each is here for the same reason: to show what evidence of a real cover-up actually looks like — a paper trail, a whistleblower, an inquiry, a named official, and a date.

1. MKUltra — the CIA’s mind-control programme (1953–1973)

Approved by CIA Director Allen Dulles in 1953 and run largely by chemist Sidney Gottlieb, MKUltra funded research into behavioural control: LSD, hypnosis, sensory deprivation, electroshock. It operated through universities, hospitals and prisons, and in many documented cases the subjects did not consent.

Its most notorious strand was Operation Midnight Climax: CIA-linked safe houses where men were dosed without their knowledge and observed through two-way mirrors.

The human cost has a name. Frank Olson, an Army scientist at Fort Detrick, was secretly given LSD at a November 1953 meeting and died days later in a fall from a New York hotel window. His family had the body exhumed in 1994; a forensic report found a head injury that may have preceded the fall. Prosecutors investigated for five years and could not prove murder — so the manner of death remains disputed even though the dosing does not.

How it was proven: a 1974 New York Times investigation, followed by the 1977 Senate Church Committee hearings. Roughly 149 subprojects are documented — and the record is incomplete because Richard Helms ordered the files destroyed in 1973.

2. COINTELPRO — the FBI against its own citizens (1956–1971)

The FBI’s counter-intelligence programme surveilled, infiltrated and disrupted political groups: the Communist Party USA, the Socialist Workers Party, the Black Panther Party, the Southern Christian Leadership Conference — and Martin Luther King Jr. personally. On 21 November 1964 an anonymous “suicide package” was mailed to King, assembled by the Bureau.

How it was proven: in March 1971 a group calling itself the Citizens’ Commission to Investigate the FBI burgled the Bureau’s Media, Pennsylvania office and mailed the files to journalists. The programme was exposed, and formally terminated. The Church Committee then documented its scope.

3. Tuskegee — 40 years of untreated syphilis (1932–1972)

The US Public Health Service studied the progression of syphilis in 399 Black men in Macon County, Alabama, without telling them their diagnosis. Even after penicillin became the standard cure in the 1940s, treatment was withheld so the study could continue. It ran for forty years.

How it was proven: a 1972 Associated Press story by Jean Heller, then a federal panel, congressional hearings, and a $10 million settlement. President Clinton formally apologised in 1997.

One correction matters, because the popular version exaggerates it: the men were not deliberately infected. A separate programme in Guatemala (1946–48), documented by a presidential commission in 2011, did intentionally expose people to sexually transmitted infections without consent. Both were abuses; they were not the same abuse.

4. The Gulf of Tonkin — the second attack that did not happen (1964)

On 4 August 1964, the Johnson administration reported that North Vietnamese vessels had attacked two US destroyers in the Gulf of Tonkin. Congress passed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution three days later, authorising military action in Southeast Asia — the legal basis for the escalation of the Vietnam War.

The second attack did not occur. Communications were misread under pressure, and the intelligence was presented with more confidence than it deserved.

How it was proven: the Pentagon Papers (1971) and, decisively, declassified NSA signals-intelligence material. The NSA has published its own internal history, “Skunks, Bogies, Silent Hounds, and the Flying Fish: The Gulf of Tonkin Mystery, 2–4 August 1964”, along with the original intercepted traffic.

5. Iran–Contra (1985–1987)

Senior National Security Council staff — principally Lt. Col. Oliver North, with National Security Adviser John Poindexter — arranged secret arms sales to Iran while the US publicly refused to negotiate with hostage-takers, then diverted the proceeds to the Contras in Nicaragua after Congress had explicitly banned military aid to them (the Boland Amendment).

How it was proven: exposure in a Lebanese magazine in November 1986, then the Tower Commission, congressional hearings and Independent Counsel Lawrence Walsh’s investigation. Several convictions followed; some were later overturned on immunity grounds.

6. The tobacco industry (1950s–1998)

Internal industry documents show that companies understood the cancer risk and the addictiveness of nicotine while publicly funding doubt, attacking the science, and marketing “safer” cigarettes. The strategy was explicit: manufacture uncertainty and sell it.

How it was proven: litigation discovery, most famously the 1994 release of millions of pages from Brown & Williamson and others, and the 1998 Master Settlement Agreement. The documents are now a public archive used by historians and epidemiologists.

The pattern — and why it matters for the rest of this archive

Every case above has the same four features: a specific claim, a named institution, a paper trail that survived, and a mechanism of exposure — a burglary, a leak, a lawsuit, a congressional subpoena.

Now compare that with the claims in the other dossiers. “The Earth is hollow” has no memo. “A second sun is hidden” has no budget line. “The ice wall is guarded” has no unit, no payroll and no defector. Six decades of real conspiracies have produced tens of thousands of pages of documentation; the cosmology claims have produced photographs of a rock.

This is not a rhetorical trick. It is the strongest argument in the archive: we know what a cover-up looks like, because we have the files.

Two more worth knowing

  • Operation Northwoods (1962) — a Joint Chiefs proposal to stage attacks on US targets to justify invading Cuba. It was proposed and rejected, declassified in 1997. Real document; never executed. A useful reminder that “they discussed it” is not “they did it”.
  • Mass surveillance — the NSA’s bulk collection programmes, disclosed by Edward Snowden in 2013, were subsequently reviewed by courts and Congress and partly curtailed. A genuine secret, revealed by a named source with documents.

7. Operation Mockingbird — the CIA and the press

The claim, made for decades and dismissed as paranoia: the CIA had penetrated American news organisations and used journalists to place propaganda.

It was substantially confirmed. The 1975–76 Church Committee investigations into intelligence activities opened the archive, and in 1977 the journalist Carl Bernstein published “The CIA and the Media” in Rolling Stone, based on interviews and internal documents, describing a programme to recruit and cultivate journalists, editors and publishers — with figures in the hundreds, including people at major newspapers, magazines, and broadcasters.

The nuance matters as much as the confirmation. Much of the relationship was “friendly” cultivation rather than formal employment: a journalist who would take a call, plant a story, or hold a line. Some were on the payroll. The point is not that every story was dictated; it is that a secret channel existed, was used, and was concealed from readers — which is exactly what critics had alleged.

8. The Nayirah testimony — a war sold with a story

In October 1990, a 15-year-old girl identified only as “Nayirah” testified before a Congressional human rights caucus that Iraqi soldiers had entered a Kuwaiti hospital, removed babies from incubators and left them to die on the floor. The testimony was credited with helping shift American public opinion toward war.

It was arranged. Nayirah was the daughter of the Kuwaiti ambassador to the United States, and the hearings had been organised with the help of Hill & Knowlton, a public relations firm working for a Kuwaiti-funded group. The incubator story was promoted as research; later investigations found no evidence for it, and reporters established that the witness's identity had been concealed deliberately.

The episode is now a standard case study in how a democracy can be moved to war by a manufactured story told in an official setting — and, notably, it was exposed by journalists rather than by any internal check.

9. The Business Plot — a coup offered to a general

In 1933 the retired Marine Corps Major General Smedley Butler — the most decorated Marine of his era, and by then a fierce critic of war profiteering — told a congressional committee that a group of wealthy men had approached him to lead a fascist veterans' organisation and march on Washington, in effect a coup against President Roosevelt.

The House committee chaired by John McCormack investigated, took testimony, and reported that it had found evidence of an attempt to establish a fascist organisation and that the plotters had approached Butler. The alleged go-between was Gerald MacGuire, a bond salesman for a Wall Street firm. No one was prosecuted.

Historians differ on the scale — how far the money went, how serious the operational planning was, whether some witnesses exaggerated. What is documented is that the approach happened, that Butler reported it, and that a congressional committee took it seriously enough to write it up. That is the useful middle case: not a fantasy, not a fully executed plot, but a real attempt that failed and was then investigated openly.

10. Watergate — the conspiracy that ended a presidency

In June 1972 five men were arrested burgling the Democratic National Committee offices in the Watergate building. Two Washington Post reporters, Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein, followed the money and the connections toward the White House while the administration dismissed the story as partisan invention.

The cover-up collapsed through documented evidence: the Senate hearings in 1973, the revelation of the White House taping system, and the tapes themselves. The Supreme Court ordered them released in July 1974; Nixon resigned in August.

Watergate is the benchmark for this whole dossier. It shows what a real conspiracy looks like from the outside — an actual crime, actual concealment, and a trail of documents, money and recordings that eventually outran the people hiding them. Every claim in these pages that is filed as Documented had to meet that standard. Every claim filed as Debunked failed it.

11. Operation Paperclip — the scientists who were hired

After the Second World War, the United States recruited German scientists and engineers — including Wernher von Braun, who had led the V-2 rocket programme — to work on American rockets and weapons. The programme was called Operation Paperclip.

The part that was rumoured and then confirmed is the paperwork: officials removed or softened Nazi Party membership and war-crime connections from the personnel files of recruits so they could be cleared to enter the country. More than 1,600 people were brought over, and their work fed directly into the US space programme and the missile arsenal.

The moral accounting is genuinely contested, and the archive does not pretend otherwise. Von Braun's V-2 was built with forced labour, and more people died making it than were killed by it. The programme's defenders argue that the alternative was Soviet recruitment; its critics argue that justice was traded for technical advantage. What is not contested is that the recruitment happened and the records were sanitised — which is the definition of a cover-up, documented in the government's own files.

Appendix: not a plot, a vindication — Semmelweis and the invisible particles

Some of the best evidence that the establishment can be catastrophically wrong comes from medicine, and the standard case is Ignaz Semmelweis.

In 1847, working at the Vienna General Hospital, Semmelweis noticed that the maternity ward staffed by doctors had a far higher death rate from puerperal fever than the ward staffed by midwives. The doctors came to the ward straight from autopsies. He ordered handwashing in chlorinated lime solution, and mortality in his ward fell sharply — in his reported figures, from around 10% to around 1–2%.

He was not believed. His explanation — “cadaveric particles” carried on hands — had no mechanism anyone accepted, his manner was combative, and he published nothing for years. He lost his post, and in 1865 he was committed to an asylum, where he died at 47 of an infection from a wound.

Germ theory arrived afterwards: Pasteur's work in the 1860s, Koch's identification of specific pathogens in the 1870s–80s. Semmelweis was vindicated, decades too late, and the phrase “Semmelweis reflex” now names the rejection of evidence because it conflicts with consensus.

Why this belongs in this dossier and not in a conspiracy one

Nobody was conspiring. The doctors who rejected Semmelweis were not hiding data; they were reasoning inside a framework that had no place for invisible contagion. That is the more common failure, and the more instructive one: institutional resistance is usually not a plot — it is a paradigm with inertia.

The archive keeps both cases because they are often confused. MKUltra above was a conspiracy: a secret programme, hidden from the public, exposed by documents. Semmelweis was a failure of consensus, exposed by replication. Telling them apart is the difference between history and the stories people tell about history.
