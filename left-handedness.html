Why Ten Per Cent Are Left-Handed

Hand preference is stable, partly heritable, and not explained. Identical twins disagree about a third of the time, which rules out a simple genetic account.

The pattern

About one in ten people is left-handed, the proportion is stable across cultures and centuries, and it is higher in men than in women. Hand preference appears early — thumb-sucking preference in the womb predicts it — and it is remarkably stable over a life.

Maps, diagrams and images

The explanations

There is a genetic component: left-handedness runs in families, and several genes have been associated with it, including LRRTM1. But the heritability is modest, and monozygotic twins are discordant for handedness in roughly a quarter to a third of pairs. Identical genes, different hands.

That discordance points to developmental noise — random variation in the developing brain, perhaps in the timing of lateralisation — rather than a deterministic programme. Handedness looks like a threshold trait: many small influences, a cut-off, and randomness at the boundary.

The evolutionary question is why left-handers persist at all. The leading suggestion is a frequency-dependent advantage in combat or sport, where a minority of left-handers has an advantage that disappears if the minority grows.

Where it stands

Documented: the prevalence, the early onset, the stability and the sex difference.

Documented: a genetic contribution, and discordance in identical twins.

Not established: a single cause, or a complete model.

Contested: the advantage that maintains the minority.

The honest label: live debate — a well-measured phenomenon with no settled explanation.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the everyday version of the archive's recurring lesson: a trait that looks like it must have one cause, and turns out to have several, one of which is chance.
