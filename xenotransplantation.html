Xenotransplantation

Transplanting pig organs into people. The first living recipients survived weeks to months, which is both a real advance and a long way from a treatment.

The idea

Xenotransplantation uses animal organs — almost always pig — for human transplant, to address the shortage of donor organs. Pigs are used because their organs are a similar size and they breed quickly. The barrier is immunological: the human immune system rejects pig tissue violently, and pigs carry endogenous retroviruses that must be edited out.

Maps, diagrams and images

The results

In 2022 and 2024, gene-edited pig hearts and kidneys were transplanted into a small number of living patients in the United States, under compassionate-use arrangements. The first recipients died within about two months; a later kidney recipient lived longer. Two brain-dead patients were also used for short studies.

These are genuine firsts and they are not a therapy. The survival times are short, the patients were critically ill, and the trials were not controlled.

What is established

Documented: pig-to-human transplants have been performed, with gene-edited organs.

Documented: survival for weeks to months in a handful of cases.

Not established: a reliable, durable treatment.

Contested: the risk of cross-species infection, and the ethics of compassionate use.

The honest label: documented as an experimental procedure, not as a treatment.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is a real medical frontier where the honest description — a handful of compassionate-use cases — is much less exciting than the coverage, and the coverage is what most people will remember.
