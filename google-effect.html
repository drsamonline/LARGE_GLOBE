The Google Effect

People remember where to find information rather than the information itself. The 2011 paper that named it is famous; the replications are mixed, which is the honest state of the finding.

The finding

Betsy Sparrow, Jenny Liu and Daniel Wegner published 'Google Effects on Memory' in Science in 2011. Across experiments, participants who expected to have future access to information remembered the information less well and the location of it better. The paper framed this as transactive memory extended to the internet: the same process by which people rely on a partner or a library.

The caveats

The effect sizes were modest and the replications have been mixed. Some studies found the 'remember where, not what' pattern; others found no difference, or found that the expectation manipulation did not work as intended. A 2021 replication attempt reported weaker and less consistent effects.

The popular version — 'the internet is making us stupid' — is not what the paper says. Transactive memory is a normal strategy, and there is no evidence that relying on external stores reduces general cognitive ability.

What is established

Documented: people use external stores as memory, and have always done so.

Reported: a shift toward remembering locations when future access is expected.

Mixed: replications, with weaker effects than the original.

Not established: any decline in intelligence or memory ability.

The honest label: documented as a phenomenon, with the strength of the effect still being settled.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the rare case where the popular reading is more pessimistic than the evidence, and because transactive memory is one of the few well-studied ways that minds extend into the world.
