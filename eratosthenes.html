Eratosthenes Measured the Earth

A librarian in Alexandria calculated the circumference of the Earth from a shadow and a distance, and got within a few per cent. The method is documented.

The measurement

Eratosthenes, chief librarian at Alexandria in the third century BC, knew that at Syene (modern Aswan) the sun was directly overhead at noon on the summer solstice — a well in the town was lit to the bottom. At Alexandria, on the same day, a gnomon cast a shadow, giving an angle of about 7.2 degrees, roughly a fiftieth of a circle. The distance between the two places was known from surveys.

The result

Multiplying the distance by fifty gave a circumference within a few per cent of the modern value, depending on which stade he used — the uncertainty is in the unit, not the method. The calculation assumes the sun's rays are parallel, which is a good approximation at that scale.

The work survives through reports by later writers, and the method is sound.

What is established

Documented: the method and the observations.

Documented: the result, within a few per cent.

Uncertain: the exact length of the stade used.

Documented: the transmission through later writers.

The label: documented.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the clearest demonstration that ancient people measured the world accurately, which is the fact that most of the previous shelves manage to avoid.
