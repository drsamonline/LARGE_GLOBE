The Zoo Hypothesis

If the galaxy is full of life, why is it quiet? John Ball's 1973 answer was that they are leaving us alone on purpose — treating Earth as a preserve, and waiting for us to grow up.

The proposal

John Ball's paper in Icarus in 1973 set out what he called the zoo hypothesis: extraterrestrial intelligence is common, but it follows a policy of non-interference with emerging civilisations, so that we observe nothing.

Maps, diagrams and images

The hypothesis is a solution to the Fermi paradox that accepts the premises — abundant life, capable civilisations, a galaxy old enough for them to have spread — and explains the absence of evidence as a choice rather than a scarcity.

Variants include the 'interdict' scenario, in which a local advanced civilisation enforces quarantine, and the laboratory hypothesis, in which Earth is an experiment. All share the structure: the silence is deliberate.

Why it is hard to defend

The coordination problem is severe. A policy of non-contact would have to be adopted and maintained by every civilisation in the galaxy, independently, for billions of years — across species with different biologies, values and expansion pressures. One defector, one curious probe, and the hypothesis fails.

It is also close to unfalsifiable. Any absence of evidence is consistent with it, and so is almost any future evidence: a detection could be read as the zoo opening, a continued silence as the policy holding. A hypothesis that predicts nothing in particular is cheap.

There is a testable edge, though. A strict non-interference policy need not mean invisibility: waste heat, probes, and artefacts in the solar system would all be detectable, and searches for them have found nothing so far. That is weak evidence against the strong form.

How it sits with the other answers

Compared with the Great Filter, the zoo hypothesis is optimistic: it says civilisations survive and multiply, and choose restraint. The Filter says they mostly do not survive.

Compared with dark forest, it is the opposite conclusion from similar premises: silence as courtesy rather than as concealment.

Its strongest argument is that it needs no unknown physics — only a policy, which is the kind of thing civilisations can have.

Its weakest point is the same thing: a universal policy sustained without enforcement is not a mechanism, it is a hope.

The archive labels this fringe because it is a serious published proposal with no evidence for it and no clear way to test it — an entry in the solution space, not a finding.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the most humane of the Fermi solutions and the least constrained by evidence, and because the difference between those two facts is worth seeing clearly.
