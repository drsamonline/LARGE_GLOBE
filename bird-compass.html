The Quantum Biology of the Bird Compass

Migratory birds navigate using the Earth's magnetic field, and the leading explanation involves quantum entanglement in a protein in the retina. It is one of the best-supported examples of quantum biology.

The phenomenon

Many migratory birds can detect the direction of the Earth's magnetic field, and the sense depends on light: in the 1970s, experiments showed that the orientation of European robins under artificial magnetic fields broke down when the wavelength of light changed, and that the effect was in the right eye.

Maps, diagrams and images

The mechanism

The leading model is the radical-pair mechanism. When light strikes a protein called cryptochrome in the retina, it creates a pair of radicals whose spins are quantum-entangled; the Earth's field alters the interconversion between spin states, and the resulting chemical signal depends on the field's direction.

The evidence is now substantial: the light dependence, the magnetic sensitivity of cryptochrome in vitro, and 2021 work showing that the protein responds to magnetic fields in a way consistent with the model. Whether the bird's nervous system reads that signal is the remaining question.

What is established

Documented: magnetic orientation in birds, and its light dependence.

Supported: the radical-pair mechanism in cryptochrome.

Contested: the exact neural readout.

Not established: the complete pathway.

The label: documented — a real quantum effect in a real behaviour.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the legitimate end of quantum biology, and because the 'quantum' pages that follow it in this collection are borrowing its credibility.
