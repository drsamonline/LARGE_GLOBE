Déjà Vu

The feeling that this has happened before, with no memory of when. Most people have it, nobody has a settled explanation, and the one thing it is not is evidence of a previous life.

The experience

Déjà vu is the sensation that a present experience has been lived before, combined with the knowledge that it has not. Around two-thirds of people report it, most often in the teens and twenties, and it tends to fade with age. It is brief, usually a few seconds, and often accompanied by a sense of significance.

The explanations

The main accounts are memory-based: a feeling of familiarity is generated without the accompanying recollection, and the brain interprets the mismatch as 'this happened before'. Candidate mechanisms include a brief double-processing of the same input, misattribution of a familiar scene from a film or a dream, and temporal-lobe activity — déjà vu is a common symptom in temporal-lobe epilepsy, which gives the research a clinical anchor.

The alternatives — previous lives, parallel universes, precognition — have no evidence and no test. The experience is a strong one, which is why it attracts them.

What is established

Documented: the experience is common, brief, and declines with age.

Documented: it is associated with temporal-lobe activity and appears in epilepsy.

Contested: the precise mechanism; several memory accounts compete.

Not established: any connection to past lives, timelines or precognition.

The honest label: live debate about mechanism, with the fringe readings having no support.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the most universal of the strange experiences the archive covers, and because it is the one where the difference between a feeling and an inference is easiest to see.
