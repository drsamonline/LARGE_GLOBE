Stuxnet

A computer worm that destroyed uranium centrifuges in Iran by making them spin at the wrong speed while reporting normal operation. It is the first known cyber weapon to cause physical damage.

The worm

Stuxnet was discovered in 2010. It spread through Windows systems, but its payload targeted Siemens industrial controllers of a specific configuration used in uranium enrichment. It recorded normal operating data, replayed it to the monitoring system, and meanwhile drove the centrifuges through damaging speed cycles.

Maps, diagrams and images

Analyses found that it used four zero-day exploits — unprecedented at the time — and that it was designed to reach one specific facility, widely identified as Natanz in Iran. It is generally attributed to the United States and Israel, though neither has officially confirmed it.

The consequences

The operation damaged or destroyed a substantial number of centrifuges and delayed the programme. It also demonstrated that code could destroy physical equipment, and it changed how governments thought about critical infrastructure.

What is established

Documented: the worm, its payload and its effects.

Documented: the zero-days and the target configuration.

Attributed: to the US and Israel, by reporting rather than by official confirmation.

Documented: physical damage to centrifuges.

The label: documented.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the clearest case of a real operation that sounds like fiction, and because the technical detail is public and checkable.
