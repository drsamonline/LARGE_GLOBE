Why We Yawn

Yawning is contagious, appears before birth, and has no agreed function. The brain-cooling account is the best supported; the social account explains the contagion.

The phenomenon

Yawning is a reflex — mouth opening, deep inhalation, stretching — found in mammals and birds, and present in fetuses from about eleven weeks. In humans it is contagious: seeing, hearing or reading about a yawn makes one more likely, and the contagion is stronger for people you know and weaker for strangers.

The theories

The brain-cooling hypothesis (Gallup and colleagues) says a yawn thermoregulates: it draws cool air in and increases blood flow, and yawning is more frequent when ambient temperature is in a comfortable range and less frequent at extremes. The arousal hypothesis says yawning is a state change that increases alertness. The social hypothesis says contagious yawning is a synchronisation signal.

The evidence is mixed and the field is small. The cooling account has the best experimental support; the contagion is real and correlates with empathy measures, but the correlation is modest and does not establish the function.

Where it stands

Documented: the reflex, its prenatal presence, and its contagion in humans.

Supported: the brain-cooling account, in some experimental settings.

Contested: whether contagion reflects empathy, and what it is for.

Not established: any single function.

The honest label: live debate — a universal behaviour with no agreed explanation.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the smallest possible open question, and because reading this page will probably make you yawn — which is the phenomenon doing its own demonstration.
