Quantum Oddities

Two of the strangest things physicists have said about electrons and death: you can never experience your own, and there may only be one electron in the universe.

1. Quantum immortality

Take many-worlds seriously: the wavefunction never collapses, and every measurement outcome happens in some branch. Now apply it to a fatal event. In one branch you die; in another you survive, having seen something slightly different. From the inside, you can only ever be in the branch where you are still there to notice.

So, the argument goes, your subjective experience never terminates. You keep finding yourself in the surviving branch — indefinitely. Max Tegmark discussed this in a 1998 paper on many-worlds, and it is usually called quantum suicide when framed as a thought experiment.

It is a genuinely interesting consequence to think through, and it is also where the reasoning quietly breaks.

Where quantum immortality fails

It assumes a continuous “you” across branches. In many-worlds there is no single observer travelling between outcomes; there are copies, each with a history. The one that dies does not experience the survival of the other. Calling the survivor “you” is a choice of words, not a physical continuity.

It throws away probability. If you are guaranteed to survive, the Born rule — the part of quantum mechanics that gives correct predictions and has passed every test — becomes irrelevant to your biography. A theory that must discard its most successful predictive rule to save a conclusion is in trouble.

Branching is not a health insurance policy. Survival in a branch requires a branch where survival is physically possible. Ageing is not a quantum measurement; it is thermodynamic, and there is no branch where entropy runs backwards for you.

It is unfalsifiable. Anyone reporting from a branch where they died is, by construction, unavailable for interview. The same structure as Last Thursdayism, and the same problem.

One honest note: the thought experiment is sometimes discussed alongside actual suicide, which is why physicists who write about it tend to be careful. It is a claim about interpretation, not an encouragement, and it has no clinical content whatsoever.

2. The one-electron universe

In 1940, John Archibald Wheeler called Richard Feynman with an idea. Every electron might be the same electron — a single particle travelling forward and backward in time, appearing at every point in space where an electron can be, and appearing as a positron when it moves backwards.

The appeal is real: a positron is, in the mathematics of the Dirac equation, an electron with reversed time direction. Wheeler's picture turns the apparent multiplicity of particles into one worldline that crosses the universe repeatedly.

Feynman took the idea seriously enough to build his positron theory around the time-reversal symmetry, and acknowledged Wheeler in the work. The picture is beautiful, and it does not survive as a description of nature.

Why it is a historical curiosity, not a result

It cannot explain the number of electrons. The universe contains something like 10⁸⁰ electrons. A single worldline crossing time repeatedly can produce many appearances, but the theory gives no reason for the observed ratio of electrons to positrons, or for the specific, stable particle content of the Standard Model.

Feynman himself moved on. The path-integral formulation that made him famous treats antiparticles as reversed-time particles without requiring that they all be the same particle. The insight survived; the identity claim did not.

Quantum field theory replaced the picture. In QFT, particles are excitations of fields, and “the same electron” is not a well-defined notion across the universe. Asking whether two electrons are the same particle is like asking whether two waves in the sea are the same wave.

Why both belong here

Because they are the archive's two failure modes of popular physics, side by side. Quantum immortality is a consequence that gets stated as a fact while its premises are quietly dropped. The one-electron universe is a beautiful hypothesis that was abandoned by the people who made it, and is now retold as if it were still live.

The test the archive applies is the same both times: does anyone who works on this treat it as true, and would anything count as evidence against it? For many-worlds itself, the answer is “serious people, and it's an interpretation”. For these two, the answer is “no, and no”.
