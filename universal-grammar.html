Universal Grammar

The claim that children are born with an innate language faculty that constrains the possible grammars. It reshaped linguistics, and the evidence for the strong version has weakened.

The claim

Chomsky's universal grammar holds that the human capacity for language is innate and structured: children are not taught grammar, they acquire it from limited input, so there must be an inborn faculty constraining the space of possible grammars.

Maps, diagrams and images

The evidence

Support comes from the poverty of the stimulus argument, from the uniformity of acquisition despite varied input, and from the existence of creoles with consistent structure. The objections are substantial: the languages of the world vary more than the theory predicted, construction-based and usage-based accounts explain acquisition without an innate grammar, and the 'language organ' has no identified neural or genetic basis.

The debate is live, and the field has partly moved on: the strong nativist version is defended by some and abandoned by many.

Where it stands

Documented: rapid acquisition from limited input.

Contested: whether an innate grammar is required.

Contested: the extent of cross-linguistic universals.

Not identified: a neural or genetic basis for the faculty.

The honest label: live debate.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is one of the most consequential theories in the humanities, and because its strongest form is now contested from inside the field.
