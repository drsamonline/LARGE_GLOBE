Quantum Tunnelling in Smell

The claim that we smell by detecting molecular vibrations through quantum tunnelling, rather than by shape. The evidence is mixed and the debate is live.

The idea

The standard account of smell is shape-based: molecules fit receptors, and the fit determines the odour. In 1996 Luca Turin proposed a vibration-based alternative: the receptor detects the vibrational frequency of the molecule through inelastic electron tunnelling, which would explain why molecules with similar shapes but different vibrations smell different.

Maps, diagrams and images

The tests

The critical test uses isotopes: a molecule with deuterium instead of hydrogen has almost the same shape but a different vibrational frequency. If smell is vibration-based, the two should smell different. Some experiments found a difference in flies and humans; others found none, and the results have been contested on both sides.

The consensus is that shape does most of the work and that a vibrational contribution is possible but unproven.

Where it stands

Documented: the shape-based account, which explains most of the data.

Proposed: the tunnelling account, with a specific test.

Contested: the isotope experiments, in both directions.

Not established: a vibrational mechanism in human olfaction.

The honest label: live debate.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is a rare case of a fringe-sounding idea from a serious scientist being tested properly, and remaining undecided.
