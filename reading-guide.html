How to Read This Archive

New here? Start with how to read a dossier

186 dossiers is a lot to walk into. This page gives you a route through them, explains the four labels you will see everywhere, and shows how to check any claim yourself in about a minute.

Ten minutes

The shortest useful visit

One dossier, one chart, one source list. Enough to see whether the archive's way of working is for you.

 An evening

The method, then three cases

Read the evidence labels, then a documented case, a live debate and a debunking, in that order.

 A week

The whole shelf

Follow a theme end to end, then take the master list and the single-volume edition with you.

Ten minutes: one claim, properly

Pick something you already have an opinion about. Flat Earth is the best test case in the archive, because the evidence is something you can check with a stick, a friend and a phone. Read the dossier to the end — past the claim, past the response, into the source list — then open one of those sources and see whether it says what the page says it says.

If you only look at one other thing, make it the numbers page: how the 150 claims in the master list are labelled, and how much sourcing stands behind each dossier. Numbers are a fast honesty check on any publication, including this one.

An evening: the four labels

Every claim in this archive carries one of four labels. They are not a scale of how interesting a claim is, and they are not a judgement of the people who believe it. They describe the state of the evidence.

Read in this order for the clearest contrast: Proven Conspiracies (documented), Lead and Crime (live debate), Hollow Earth (fringe), the Cardiff Giant (debunked with a receipt).

A week: pick a theme, follow it

The archive is now sorted into seven themes, each with its own page: browse the taxonomy. A theme is the right unit for a long visit, because neighbouring dossiers share evidence. Reading the Ice Wall, Hollow Earth and the Larger Globe together teaches you more than the same three read apart.

For the completist: the master list is 150 claims with one verdict each and a filter; the single-volume edition collects every dossier into one page you can print.

Anatomy of a dossier

Every page is built the same way, so you can read the structure rather than hunting for it.

  1. Kicker and label. Where and when the claim comes from, and its evidence label.
  2. Standfirst. The claim in one sentence, fairly stated.
  3. The claim, in its own terms. Named proponents and their strongest argument, not a straw man.
  4. The response. What the evidence actually shows, with links to the tests.
  5. What remains open. The honest residue — every page has one.
  6. Sources. Every claim traceable to a named, linked source at the foot of the page.
  7. Facts panel. Dates, status and the one detail worth remembering.

Five questions for any claim

These work on this archive as well as on anything else you read.

  • What would change my mind? If nothing could, it is not a claim about the world.
  • Who says so, and how would they know? A named source with access beats an anonymous authority.
  • Is the evidence primary or second-hand? A photograph, a file, a measurement — or a retelling of a retelling?
  • Does the explanation need a cover-up to survive? If yes, the bar for evidence goes up, not down.
  • What does the mainstream account actually say? Not the version in the conspiracy video — the version in the paper.
The one rule

Nothing here asks you to believe anything. Every page ends with the sources it used, so the archive is a set of arguments you can check rather than a set of conclusions you have to accept.
