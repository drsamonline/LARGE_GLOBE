Salting Carthage

Rome destroyed Carthage in 146 BC, and the story says the ground was sown with salt so nothing would grow. The salt is a modern invention.

The destruction

In 146 BC, at the end of the Third Punic War, Roman forces under Scipio Aemilianus destroyed Carthage: the city was taken, its population enslaved, and the site was formally cursed and abandoned. The destruction is well documented.

Maps, diagrams and images

The salt

The salting story does not appear in the ancient sources. It emerges in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, in histories and textbooks, as a vivid way of expressing total destruction. The nearest ancient references are to ritual cursing and to the symbolic ploughing of the site, not to salt.

The practical objection is also decisive: salting a city's fields at scale would require enormous quantities of salt, and the site was later resettled and became a major Roman city.

What is established

Documented: the destruction and the cursing of the site.

Not documented: any salting.

Documented: the later Roman colony on the site.

The origin: modern historians' imagery.

The label: debunked.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is a modern invention that everyone believes, and because the resettlement of the site is the plainest refutation.
