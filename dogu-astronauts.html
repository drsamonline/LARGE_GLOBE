Dogū as Astronauts

Clay figurines from Jōmon Japan have goggle-like eyes and patterned bodies. They were read as space suits. They are stylised figures, mostly female, and many were deliberately broken.

The objects

Dogū are clay figurines from the Jōmon period of Japan, made from roughly 14,000 to 300 BC. They are small, usually 10–30 cm, with exaggerated eyes, patterned surfaces and unusual proportions. They are found in domestic and burial contexts, and many are deliberately broken.

Maps, diagrams and images

The claim

The ancient-astronaut literature read them as figures in space suits, with goggles and breathing apparatus. The reading is a modern projection onto a stylised form: the 'goggles' are the goggle-eyed style of Jōmon depiction, the 'suit' is incised pattern, and the 'helmet' is a hairstyle or headdress.

The archaeological reading is that most are female, that they relate to fertility, childbirth and the household, and that the deliberate breakage is part of their use — a pattern seen in many figurine traditions.

What is established

Documented: the figurines, their dates, their contexts and their breakage.

Supported: a fertility and household association, with most figures female.

Not established: any connection to space travel.

The error: recognising a modern shape in a stylised ancient form.

The pattern: the same one as the Saqqara bird and the Quimbaya figures.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the Japanese entry in a genre that appears on every continent, and because the objects are genuinely strange without needing to be alien.
