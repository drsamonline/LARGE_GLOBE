The Dancing Plague of 1518

In Strasbourg, hundreds of people danced for days without stopping, and some died. The two leading explanations are ergot poisoning and mass psychogenic illness.

The event

In July 1518, a woman in Strasbourg began dancing in the street and did not stop. Within a week, dozens had joined; within a month, according to contemporary accounts, several hundred people were dancing. Some collapsed and a number are said to have died from exhaustion, stroke or heart attack.

Maps, diagrams and images

The town authorities responded by encouraging the dancing — building a stage and hiring musicians — on the theory that the affliction was a natural excess that had to be danced out.

The explanations

Ergot poisoning is the chemical candidate: ergot alkaloids from contaminated rye can cause convulsions and hallucinations, and the period had seen crop failures. The objection is that ergotism produces gangrene and seizures, not sustained coordinated dancing, and that the outbreak was too localised.

Mass psychogenic illness is the other candidate, supported by the period's extreme stress — famine, disease, and the belief that a saint could inflict a dancing curse.

Where it stands

Documented: the event, the location, the response of the authorities.

Plausible: mass psychogenic illness, given the documented stress and belief context.

Contested: ergot poisoning, on the symptom mismatch.

Not established: a single cause.

The honest label: live debate.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the best-documented historical outbreak of collective behaviour, and because both explanations are still argued by people who know the sources.
