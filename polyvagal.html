Polyvagal Theory

A theory that the vagus nerve supports three hierarchical states — social engagement, fight-or-flight, shutdown — and that trauma traps people in the lower ones. It is enormously popular in therapy and weakly supported in physiology.

The theory

Stephen Porges proposed polyvagal theory in 1994. It holds that the mammalian vagus has two branches: a myelinated ventral branch supporting social engagement, and an unmyelinated dorsal branch associated with shutdown, with the sympathetic system's fight-or-flight between them. Trauma is said to leave people stuck in the lower states.

Maps, diagrams and images

The criticism

Comparative anatomists have challenged the core claim: the 'two vagal systems' distinction is not supported by the anatomy of the vagus across vertebrates, and the evolutionary story attached to it does not match the evidence. The theory's clinical vocabulary — 'dorsal vagal shutdown' — is widely used but not operationalised.

Therapists report that the model is useful for talking about states of arousal, which may be true regardless of whether the physiology holds.

Where it stands

Documented: the vagus nerve and its roles in heart rate and digestion.

Contested: the two-branch hierarchy.

Not supported: the evolutionary account, per comparative anatomy.

Useful clinically: as a metaphor, according to practitioners.

The honest label: fringe — popular, useful to some, and not supported physiologically.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the most influential theory in trauma therapy that most physiologists do not accept, and because the distinction between a useful metaphor and a mechanism matters.
