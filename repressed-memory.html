Repressed Memory

The claim that traumatic memories can be pushed out of awareness and recovered later, often in therapy. The evidence for the mechanism is weak, and the recovered memories have produced both convictions and miscarriages of justice.

The claim

Repression, from Freud, holds that threatening material is pushed out of consciousness and can be recovered. In the 1980s and 1990s, therapists used techniques — hypnosis, guided imagery, suggestion — to 'recover' memories of abuse, and a wave of accusations followed.

The evidence

Memory research supports forgetting, and supports the malleability of memory under suggestion. It does not support a mechanism that reliably buries a traumatic event and later restores it intact. Laboratory studies show that suggestions can produce confident false memories, including of events that never happened.

Trauma can be poorly remembered, and some forgetting is documented; the contested claim is that a recovered memory is more likely to be true than a constructed one. Several convictions based on recovered memories have been overturned.

Where it stands

Documented: forgetting, and the power of suggestion.

Documented: false memories can be created in the lab.

Contested: whether repression is a real mechanism.

Not established: that recovered memories are reliable.

The honest label: live debate, with serious legal consequences.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the case where a psychological theory changed lives and courtrooms, and where the evidence moved against the theory.
