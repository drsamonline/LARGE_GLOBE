The Iron Pillar of Delhi

A six-tonne iron pillar from the Gupta period that has barely rusted in 1,600 years. It is not a lost technology; it is phosphorus, and a dry climate.

The object

The pillar stands in the Qutb complex in Delhi. It is 7.2 metres tall, weighs about six tonnes, and carries a Sanskrit inscription identifying a Gupta king, dating it to the fourth or fifth century. It is roughly 99% iron, and its surface has corroded very little.

Maps, diagrams and images

The explanation

The corrosion resistance comes from a thin layer of misawite — a phosphate-containing iron compound — that forms on the surface and passivates it. The phosphorus came from the ore and the smelting process; it is not a deliberate alloying addition, and the dry Delhi climate does the rest.

The 'rustless iron' framing is the romantic one. The metallurgy is impressive for its date — a large forged pillar of high-purity iron — but it is not evidence of a lost process. The phosphorus was an accident of the ore, and the same composition in a wet coastal climate would not have survived as well.

What is established

Documented: the pillar, its date, its mass, and its low corrosion.

Documented: the phosphate layer that explains the corrosion resistance.

Documented: the phosphorus source in the ore and the smelting.

Not established: a lost rust-proofing technology, or deliberate alloying.

The honest label: documented — a remarkable object with an ordinary explanation.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the best-loved 'ancient technology' claim in India, and the answer is more interesting than the myth: an accidental alloy, a good climate, and a thousand years of people noticing.
