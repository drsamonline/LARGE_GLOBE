Aphantasia

Some people close their eyes and see nothing. The condition was described in 1880, named in 2015, and is now the best-studied example of how differently ordinary minds are furnished.

What it is

Aphantasia is the absence, or near-absence, of voluntary mental imagery. Asked to picture an apple, most people report something apple-like; a person with aphantasia reports nothing, or knows the facts about an apple without seeing one. It is not a memory deficit, not a failure of recognition, and not blindness. People with it recognise faces, navigate cities and describe scenes — they do it without the picture.

Francis Galton described the variation in 1880, in a survey of mental imagery that found, to his surprise, that some scientists reported none at all. The thread was dropped for a century. In 2015 Adam Zeman's group published a case series of people who lost imagery after surgery or stroke, and coined the term; the congenital form turned out to be far more common.

How common, and how it is measured

Estimates cluster around 1–4% of the population, measured with the Vividness of Visual Imagery Questionnaire, in which people rate the vividness of imagined scenes. Self-report is the weak point: you cannot check someone's imagery from outside, and the questionnaire measures the confidence of the report as much as the experience behind it.

Objective measures are catching up. Binocular rivalry, in which two images compete for awareness, is biased by imagining one of them — an effect that is reduced or absent in aphantasia. Pupil dilation tracks imagined brightness in most people. Brain imaging shows weaker connectivity between frontal control regions and visual cortex when people try to imagine, though the picture is not a clean on/off switch.

What is established, and what is not

Documented: congenital aphantasia exists, is not rare, and does not impair most everyday function.

Documented: imagery is not one thing. Some people lack visual imagery but have auditory or spatial imagery; some have vivid dreams and no waking imagery.

Open: what it means for memory, creativity and clinical treatment, where imagery-based therapies assume a picture the patient may not have.

Not supported: the claim that people with aphantasia have no imagination. They have no voluntary imagery. The two words are not synonyms, and conflating them is the commonest error in the popular writing.

Why it belongs here

Because it is the counterweight to every claim on this site that treats the mind as a single thing with a standard setting. The variation is real, measurable, and invisible from outside — which is also why the archive is cautious about first-person reports in both directions.
