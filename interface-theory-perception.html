The Interface Theory of Perception

The claim that perception is a desktop interface, useful but not true, and that evolution optimises for fitness rather than accuracy. It is a serious argument in the philosophy of perception with an empirical claim that has been contested.

The claim

Donald Hoffman's interface theory of perception argues that what we see is not a window on reality but a simplified interface — like desktop icons that hide the circuitry and are useful precisely because they do. Evolution selects for fitness, not for truth, and a perception that tracked reality accurately could be less fit than one that did not.

Maps, diagrams and images

The supporting work is simulation: evolutionary games in which perceptual strategies compete, and in which 'truth' strategies are reported to lose to 'fitness-only' strategies.

The objections

The simulations have been criticised for the structure of the games and for the definition of fitness. Biologists have argued that tracking real features of the environment is usually the fittest strategy, so the two come apart only in contrived cases. The theory is also hard to test: it predicts that we should not expect perception to be veridical, which is compatible with almost any observation.

Where it stands

Documented: the theory, its simulations, and its place in the philosophy of perception.

Contested: the simulation results and what they show.

Contested: whether fitness and truth generally diverge.

Not testable: directly, in the form usually stated.

The honest label: live debate — a serious philosophical position with an empirical argument that is disputed, not settled.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the archive's most respectable 'reality is not what it seems' claim, and because it shows that the difference between that and the fringe is the presence of an argument rather than a revelation.
