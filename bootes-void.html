The Boötes Void

A region of sky about 330 million light years across contains far fewer galaxies than it should. It is real, it is not empty, and it is not evidence of anything stranger than large-scale structure.

The finding

In 1981 Robert Kirshner and colleagues reported that a survey of the Boötes region found far fewer galaxies than expected for a volume of that size. The underdense region is roughly 330 million light years across — enormous, and one of the largest structures known.

Maps, diagrams and images

The press called it the Great Nothing. The measurement does not support that name: the void contains galaxies, dozens of them, just far fewer per unit volume than the cosmic average.

What it means

Voids are a prediction of structure formation. Gravity pulls matter into filaments and clusters, leaving underdense regions between them; the largest voids are hundreds of millions of light years across, and the observed distribution matches the models. The Boötes Void is a large example, not an anomaly.

It has been recruited for stranger claims — a region cleared by an advanced civilisation, a 'hole in the universe' — and those claims have no measurements behind them. The void is not empty, and its shape follows the filamentary structure around it.

What is established

Documented: an underdense region in Boötes, roughly 330 million light years across.

Documented: it contains galaxies, at a fraction of the average density.

Documented: voids of this kind are predicted by and consistent with structure formation.

Not established: emptiness, a sharp boundary, or any artificial cause.

Why it is interesting: because it is one of the largest structures we can see, and it is made of absence.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the archive's reminder that 'structure' includes holes. The universe's largest features are not only clusters but the gaps between them, and the gaps are as informative as the clusters.
