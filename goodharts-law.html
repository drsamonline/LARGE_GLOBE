Goodhart's Law

When a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. The law is a one-line summary of a century of institutional failure, and it is demonstrated every year.

The law

Charles Goodhart formulated it in 1975 about monetary aggregates: any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed on it for control purposes. Marilyn Strathern's 1997 paraphrase is the one everyone quotes: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

The examples

The pattern is old and well documented. Soviet factories given targets in tonnes produced heavy furniture; given targets in nails, they produced useless tiny ones. Wells Fargo's sales targets produced millions of fraudulent accounts and a $3 billion settlement. British hospital waiting-time targets produced gaming of the clock; police crime-recording targets produced downgrading of offences.

The mechanism is not stupidity. It is that a target creates an incentive to optimise the number rather than the thing the number was supposed to represent, and the people closest to the number are best placed to do it.

What is established

Documented: the law, its origin and its paraphrase.

Documented: the case history, across sectors and decades.

The mechanism: incentive applied to a proxy measure.

Not a law of nature: it is a pattern, not a theorem.

The label: documented.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the most useful single sentence in the collection, and because every institution on the previous shelf was, at bottom, an example of it.
