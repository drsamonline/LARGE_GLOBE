Plant Neurobiology

Plants signal, remember in a loose sense, and respond to damage. The claim that they have nervous systems, or brains, is where the metaphor stops being useful.

What is real

Plants have electrical signalling: action potentials in the Venus flytrap and in Mimosa, propagated through the phloem, and slower calcium waves. They have auxin gradients that carry information about light and gravity. They respond to damage with systemic signals, and some show priming effects that resemble memory.

Maps, diagrams and images

This is a genuine field — plant electrophysiology and plant signalling — with real results.

The claim that overreaches

Plant neurobiology, as a named programme from 2005, argued that plants have a nervous system in a meaningful sense: synapse-like structures, neurotransmitter analogues, and a 'brain-like' command centre in the root apex. Critics replied that analogy is not homology: plants have no neurons, no synapses, and no evidence of a central integrating organ, and that the vocabulary imports claims the data do not support.

Where it stands

Documented: electrical and chemical signalling in plants, and adaptive responses.

Documented: action potentials, calcium waves, auxin transport.

Not established: neurons, synapses, or a plant brain.

Contested: whether the vocabulary of neuroscience is illuminating or misleading here.

The honest label: fringe — a real biology with a metaphor that has been promoted to a claim.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the clearest case of a metaphor doing the work of a finding, and because the underlying biology is more interesting than the claim built on it.
