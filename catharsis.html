Catharsis

The idea that venting anger relieves it. The evidence says the opposite: rehearsal makes anger more accessible, and the studies that tested it found venting increases aggression.

The claim

Catharsis comes from Aristotle's account of tragedy and from Freud and Breuer's early work: strong emotion, if expressed, is discharged, and the discharge is therapeutic. In its popular form it says that venting anger — shouting, hitting something, venting online — reduces it.

The evidence

The direct tests are unkind to the hypothesis. Bushman's work in the 2000s found that participants who hit a punching bag while thinking about the person who angered them became more aggressive, not less, compared with those who sat quietly. A meta-analysis found that venting reduces neither anger nor aggression and can increase both.

What does help is different: distraction, reappraisal, and in some cases writing about the experience — which is expressive disclosure, not catharsis. The distinction matters because both get called 'getting it out'.

Where it stands

Documented: the hypothesis and its history.

Not supported: venting as a way to reduce anger.

Found instead: rehearsal increases accessibility and aggression.

Supported: distraction, reappraisal and expressive writing.

The honest label: live debate in the literature, but the popular version is debunked by the direct tests.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the most widely believed piece of psychological advice that the evidence contradicts, and because the correction is practical: stop rehearsing, start doing something else.
