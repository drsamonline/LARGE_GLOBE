The Stoned Ape

Human brains tripled in size because our ancestors ate psilocybin mushrooms, which rewired us into language, art and self-reflection. It is a wonderful story with a real pharmacology and almost no archaeology.

The claim

Terence McKenna set it out in Food of the Gods (1992). The argument, in his telling: as African hominids moved from forest to grassland, they followed herds and encountered dung-loving psilocybin mushrooms. Low doses sharpened vision and increased sex drive; higher doses produced synaesthesia, and synaesthesia is the raw material of language. Repeated over evolutionary time, this drove the expansion of the human brain and the emergence of symbolic thought.

Maps, diagrams and images

He also suggested the mushrooms “travelled” as spores, which is where the hypothesis leaves biology altogether.

What is actually true

  • Psilocybin does what he said pharmacologically. It is a serotonergic psychedelic; it alters visual processing, can produce synaesthesia-like experiences, and has measurable effects on brain network organisation.
  • Human brains did get much bigger. Over roughly two million years, hominin brain volume increased substantially — from around 600 cm³ in early Homo to about 1,350 cm³ in modern humans.
  • Diet and behaviour matter. Cooking, meat-eating, food sharing, longer childhoods, social group size and tool use all have serious, evidence-backed roles in that change.
  • Psychedelics shape culture now. There is real ethnographic and historical evidence for ritual use, and modern clinical trials for depression and addiction. That is not the same as driving evolution.
Where it fails

No archaeological trace. If mushroom use were central for hundreds of thousands of years, you would expect residues, processing tools, depictions, or at least a plausible dietary signature. Dung fungi do not preserve, but the behaviour leaves nothing at all — and the earliest direct evidence of human psychoactive plant use is much later and much thinner than the hypothesis needs.

The timescale does not fit a single cause. Brain expansion happened over ~2 million years, in pulses, across several species and continents. A single dietary factor would have to explain variation, reversals, and the fact that brain size in Homo sapiens has actually decreased slightly over the last 10,000–20,000 years.

The mechanism is a guess. “Synaesthesia is the origin of language” is a poetic claim with no developmental or neurological support; language evolution research is a large field and this is not a serious contender within it.

It is untestable as stated. There is no observation that would refute it, which is the archive's standard objection: a claim that cannot fail cannot do work.

Why it survives anyway

Because the pieces are individually true and the story is gripping: a real drug, a real brain-size change, a real mystery about language, and a charismatic teller. It also offers something the dry hypotheses do not — a sense that the human mind is stranger than a calorie-counting machine.

The standard mistake is to treat a plausible-sounding mechanism as evidence. Compare the ancient astronaut file: real monuments, real gaps in our knowledge, and a mechanism invented to fill the gap that predicts nothing.

The honest sentence

Psychedelic mushrooms have probably been part of human experience for a very long time, and they do interesting things to perception. Whether they shaped the evolution of the brain is unknown, and the specific McKenna version is not supported by the evidence we have. The interesting research question — how diet, sociality and ecology interacted in hominin brain evolution — is being worked on without him.
