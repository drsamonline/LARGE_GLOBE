The Wet-Bulb Threshold

At a wet-bulb temperature of about 35 °C, sweat stops cooling the body and a healthy adult dies within hours, even in the shade. It is a hard physical limit, and parts of the world are approaching it.

What the number means

Wet-bulb temperature is measured with a thermometer wrapped in wet cloth: it is the lowest temperature reachable by evaporation. Human cooling depends almost entirely on sweating, so the wet-bulb temperature sets a ceiling on how much heat a body can dump.

At a wet-bulb of 35 °C — roughly 35 °C at 100% humidity, or 46 °C at 50% — the theoretical limit is reached: sweat cannot cool the skin below air temperature, core temperature rises, and death follows within hours even at rest in the shade.

The evidence

The 35 °C figure comes from Sherwood and Huber (2010) and is a theoretical limit for a healthy adult at rest. Later work suggests lower thresholds for older people and for those doing physical work, and modelling studies find that brief exceedances have already occurred in South Asia and the Persian Gulf.

The threshold is not a climate projection; it is thermodynamics. What is projected is how often and where it will be reached.

What is established

Documented: the physical limit, and its dependence on humidity.

Documented: brief exceedances in some regions already recorded.

Documented: lower tolerance in the elderly and in physical work.

Projected: increasing frequency in South Asia, the Gulf and parts of Africa.

The honest label: documented — a measured physical constraint, not a forecast.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the clearest case of a number that is not disputed by anyone competent and is not acted on at the scale the number implies — which is a different kind of failure from the ones the rest of this archive documents.
