Where Earth’s Water Came From

Oceans cover most of the planet, and the planet should have boiled them off while it was forming. Something delivered water afterwards — or it was here all along.

The puzzle

The inner solar system was hot enough during planet formation that volatiles should have been driven off. Yet Earth has roughly 1.4 billion cubic kilometres of water, plus a comparable amount possibly stored in the mantle.

Delivery by comets was the obvious candidate, since comets are ice-rich. Carbonaceous asteroids are the other option, and there is a third: water bound in the material Earth accreted, or formed in the solar nebula and absorbed by dust.

The deuterium fingerprint

Water carries a signature: the ratio of deuterium to hydrogen (D/H). Comets from the Oort cloud and the Kuiper Belt have D/H values typically two to three times Earth's, which rules out their delivering most of our water unless the measurements are unrepresentative.

The Rosetta mission measured comet 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko at more than three times Earth's D/H — a Jupiter-family comet, which had been the most promising class. Carbonaceous chondrite meteorites, by contrast, have D/H ratios close to Earth's.

That shifted the consensus towards asteroids, with a possible cometary contribution of a few per cent. Sample returns from Ryugu and Bennu have since confirmed that these bodies are water-rich and chemically similar to the meteorites.

Where the uncertainty remains

How much water, how early: estimates of the total inventory differ by factors of several, and much of it may be locked in the mantle rather than the ocean.

Solar nebula contribution: some hydrogen may have come from the nebula itself and been incorporated into the mantle, which would dilute any delivery signature.

The Moon-forming impact: a giant impact would have vaporised much of Earth's water. How much survived or was re-delivered is model-dependent.

The archive's label reflects the state of play: delivery by water-rich asteroids is the leading account, with the quantity and timing genuinely open.

Why it is in the archive

Because the question is a good example of how isotope geochemistry works as evidence — a ratio in a comet measured by a spacecraft constrains a story about the oceans.
