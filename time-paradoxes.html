Time Paradoxes

Kill your grandfather and you were never born; travel back and give yourself the idea you got from yourself. These paradoxes are not puzzles about fiction. They are what happens when you take the equations of relativity seriously.

The grandfather paradox

If you could travel into your own past and prevent your grandparents from meeting, you would never be born — and so could never have travelled back. It is the cleanest statement of why backward time travel looks logically dangerous.

Maps, diagrams and images

Physicists take it seriously for a specific reason: general relativity permits solutions with closed timelike curves (CTCs) — paths through spacetime that loop back to their own past. Gödel found one in 1949, in a rotating universe; others appear in solutions for rotating black holes and traversable wormholes.

So the question is not "is it imaginable?" but "does the mathematics of relativity survive the loop?"

Novikov's self-consistency

In the 1980s, Igor Novikov and collaborators proposed a resolution: the laws of physics are self-consistent, so only those histories with no contradictions can occur. You can travel back — but something will always prevent the patricide. The gun jams, you change your mind, you were adopted.

This is not a trick; it is a constraint on solutions. Thorne and others worked through billiard-ball models in wormhole spacetimes, showing that consistent solutions exist and that inconsistent ones are excluded. The cost is that free will, in the past, becomes a strange notion: you can do what you like, but only what was already consistent.

Stephen Hawking disliked the whole business and proposed the chronology protection conjecture in 1992: the laws of physics conspire to prevent CTCs from forming, because quantum effects diverge at the boundary. It remains a conjecture. Nobody has proved it, and nobody has found a working time machine.

The honest status

No CTC has ever been observed. Every known solution requires exotic matter — negative energy density — or a universe with unphysical properties. It is a mathematical possibility, not an engineering one.

The paradox is a signal, not a refutation. When a theory permits contradictions, the usual reading is that the theory is incomplete at that boundary, not that the universe is inconsistent.

Bootstrap paradoxes are worse. An object or idea with no origin — a watch you give yourself, a song you learned from the person you taught — is not contradictory but inexplicable, which some physicists find more troubling than the grandfather.

White holes

Take the equations for a black hole and run them backwards. You get a region that nothing can enter and everything must leave — a white hole. It is not a separate theory; it is the same geometry under time reversal.

White holes appear in the full Schwarzschild solution, in some models of black hole evaporation, and in bounce cosmologies where a collapsing universe re-expands. Some popular accounts suggest the Big Bang was a white hole. That is a metaphor with a caveat: the Big Bang is not an explosion into pre-existing space, and there is no exterior from which matter could be emerging.

No white hole has ever been observed. Any would be spectacular: an object radiating matter outward from a horizon nothing can cross, and the radiation would be detectable. The absence is itself informative — it is one reason physicists expect the full theory of quantum gravity to forbid them.

What the fiction gets right

Stories that use branching timelines — travel back, create a new branch — are not doing relativity; they are switching to a many-worlds-like picture, which conveniently removes the paradox. That is a legitimate narrative choice, and it is not physics. The distinction matters because it is the same one this archive draws between the interpretation and the story built on top of it.
