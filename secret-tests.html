Secret Tests

A city sprayed with bacteria, dead infants measured for fallout, a cat with a transmitter in its skull, a gun that causes heart attacks, and a proposal to make soldiers attracted to each other. All real documents.

1. Operation Sea-Spray, 1950

In September 1950 the US Navy released clouds of Serratia marcescens and other bacteria from a ship in San Francisco Bay, to test how a city might be exposed in a biological attack. The tests were secret. Eleven people were later identified as having been exposed, and one died of a Serratia infection — though whether the spray caused it has been debated by specialists.

It was revealed in Senate hearings in 1977, alongside a set of similar military tests in American cities and subway systems. The government apologised.

2. Project Sunshine, 1953 onwards

The Atomic Energy Commission collected thousands of human tissue and bone samples — including from dead infants and stillbirths — from around the world, without consent, to measure strontium-90 from atmospheric nuclear tests. The project's existence became public in the 1990s through declassified files and journalism.

It is the clearest example of the pattern: a programme that was scientifically serious, ethically indefensible, and entirely hidden until the archive opened.

3. Acoustic Kitty

The CIA spent several years and a reported $20 million trying to turn a cat into a listening device, implanting a microphone and transmitter and wiring the animal's nervous system. The project was declassified in 2001. It failed for a simple reason: cats do not take direction, and the first field test reportedly ended with the cat being hit by a taxi.

4. The heart-attack gun

In 1975 the Church Committee was shown a CIA device that fired a tiny dart coated with a toxin — saxitoxin or a shellfish poison — designed to produce a heart attack that would be hard to attribute. It was never confirmed to have been used on a person, and the agency's own stockpiles were ordered destroyed.

5. The "gay bomb"

In 1994 a laboratory at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base submitted a proposal for a "harassment" weapon using strong pheromones to make enemy soldiers sexually irresistible to one another. It was a funding proposal, never built, and was withdrawn after publicity. It is often cited as evidence of military absurdity; it is better read as evidence of what a bad idea looks like on paper.

What these cases teach

Secret programmes are documented, not inferred. Every item above has a file, a hearing or a declassification date. That is the standard Proven Conspiracies applies to everything else in this archive.

The ethical failure is usually consent. Sea-Spray and Sunshine were not secret because the science was dubious; they were secret because the public was not asked.

Capability is not intent. A programme that built a device is not evidence of its use — a distinction conspiracy literature routinely collapses.

The line to draw

The existence of these programmes is a legitimate reason to distrust official denials. It is not a reason to believe any specific claim about present-day activity, which still needs its own evidence. The archive's rule holds: the fact that they did it before is a prior, not a proof.
