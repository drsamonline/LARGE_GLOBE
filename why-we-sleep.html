Why We Sleep

Sleep restores something, and after a century of research nobody can say precisely what. The candidates are all supported and none is sufficient.

The candidates

The restorative theory says sleep repairs what waking wears out — but the repair is not localised, and the body does much of it while awake. Synaptic homeostasis (Tononi and colleagues) says sleep renormalises synaptic strength, which is supported by molecular and electrophysiological evidence and explains why learning increases sleep need.

Maps, diagrams and images

The glymphatic hypothesis says the brain clears metabolic waste faster during sleep, which has been demonstrated in rodents and is harder to measure in humans. The immune theory notes the tight coupling between sleep and immune function. The adaptive theories say sleep keeps animals still and safe when being active is unprofitable.

What is agreed

Sleep is necessary: total deprivation is fatal in animals, and chronic restriction degrades cognition, mood and metabolic health. What is not agreed is the function, and the field is honest about it in review articles even when popular books are not.

Where it stands

Documented: sleep is necessary; deprivation has measurable costs.

Supported: synaptic renormalisation, glymphatic clearance, immune coupling.

Not established: a single function that explains sleep.

Contested: how the functions relate, and which is primary.

The honest label: live debate — one of biology's large open questions.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the most ordinary unexplained thing about us, and because the confident popular books about it are a good test of whether a reader can tell a mechanism from a hypothesis.
