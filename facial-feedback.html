Facial Feedback

Holding a pen in your teeth makes cartoons funnier, according to a 1988 study. A 17-lab replication failed to find it, and the argument since has been about what that means.

The hypothesis

Darwin and William James both suggested that the experience of emotion is partly the perception of one's own bodily state. The facial feedback hypothesis makes that concrete: adopting an expression should modulate the corresponding feeling. Strack, Martin and Stepper (1988) tested it with a pen held in the teeth (forcing a smile) or the lips (preventing one), and found that the teeth condition rated cartoons funnier.

The replication

In 2016 a large multi-lab replication — seventeen laboratories, nearly two thousand participants — found an effect in the same direction but too small to be conclusive, and the original result was outside the confidence interval. The authors of the replication concluded that the effect, if it exists, is much smaller than reported.

The original study's design also came under scrutiny for the pen-in-mouth manipulation, which may have been confounded by difficulty and discomfort. Later meta-analyses with better methods find a small but real effect, which is where the field currently sits.

Where it stands

Documented: the hypothesis, and the original study.

Not replicated at the original size: the 17-lab study found a much smaller effect.

Supported: a small effect in later meta-analyses.

Contested: the mechanism — proprioception, effort, or demand characteristics.

The honest label: live debate — a famous result that shrank under replication, which is the normal fate of famous results.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the archive's clearest illustration of what replication does to a well-loved finding, and because the corrected version — small effect, uncertain mechanism — is the honest one.
