Modern Monetary Theory

A government that borrows in a currency it prints can always pay. That is arithmetic, not ideology. What follows from it — and what does not — is where the fight is.

The claim, stated properly

Modern monetary theory descends from chartalism, the older idea that money is a creature of the state: taxes create the demand for a currency, and the state then spends it into existence. Warren Mosler, Randall Wray and Stephanie Kelton turned that into a policy framework in the 1990s and 2010s.

The core propositions are narrow. A sovereign issuer of its own floating currency cannot be forced into involuntary default, because it can always credit an account. Bond issuance is therefore not funding but an interest-rate operation — a way to drain reserves so the policy rate holds. The real constraint is inflation, not solvency.

Everything interesting follows from the third proposition. If the limit is inflation rather than a budget number, then the question becomes how much real output an economy has spare, and whether a legislature can be trusted to find that limit before prices do.

Why economists outside the school object

The standard objection is not that the arithmetic is wrong but that the constraint is not as soft as it sounds. Inflation arrives before the spare capacity is used up, expectations move, and the political cost of raising taxes or cutting spending in time is high — so 'the limit is inflation' can mean 'the limit is a crisis'.

A second objection concerns small open economies. The mechanism assumes a floating exchange rate and debt in your own currency. A country that borrows in dollars or euros is running a household budget whether or not it believes otherwise, and the emerging-market record is full of governments that discovered this suddenly.

Thomas Palley's critique accepts chartalism's history and rejects its policy conclusion, arguing that MMT understates how interest-rate policy and financial fragility interact. That is a friendly critique, which makes it more useful than the ones that simply call the whole thing fantasy.

What the record shows, and does not

Supports the diagnosis: Japan has run gross debt above 250 per cent of GDP for years with low inflation and low bond yields. The United States issued trillions in 2020–21 without a bond-market revolt.

Cuts the other way: the same 2020–21 episode produced the sharpest inflation in forty years, and the political response was rate rises and a fiscal squeeze — which is what the orthodox model predicts, not what the strong MMT version does.

Untested: the proposal to guarantee employment through a job guarantee is a real policy design with real administrative costs, and no country has run it at scale.

The archive labels this live debate because the accounting is correct, the empirical record is genuinely mixed, and the political-economy objection has never been answered rather than merely dismissed.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is a case where a technical accounting identity was turned into a political programme, and both its supporters and its critics routinely argue against a version the other side does not hold.
