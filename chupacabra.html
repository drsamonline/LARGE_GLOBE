The Chupacabra

A creature that drains livestock of blood, first reported in Puerto Rico in 1995. Every carcass that has been tested has turned out to be a coyote or dog with mange.

The reports

The chupacabra — 'goat sucker' — was first reported in Puerto Rico in 1995, after a series of livestock deaths. The original description was a bipedal creature with spines and large eyes; it spread across Latin America and the southern United States.

Maps, diagrams and images

The carcasses

Since the 2000s, people have produced carcasses identified as chupacabras. DNA testing on specimens from Texas, Michigan and elsewhere has identified them as coyotes, dogs and raccoons, often with severe mange — a condition that causes hair loss, thickened skin and a gaunt appearance that matches the descriptions.

The livestock deaths are real; the interpretation is not. The original 1995 reports were never supported by a specimen.

What is established

Documented: the reports, and the carcasses.

Documented: DNA identification of the carcasses as known animals with mange.

Not established: any unknown species.

Explained: the sightings, by a common disease on common animals.

The honest label: debunked — the specimens were tested, and they answered.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the cleanest cryptid case where physical evidence existed and settled the question, and the settlement was unglamorous.
