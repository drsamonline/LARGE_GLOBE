Aliens, Area 51 & the UFO Files

The United States government spent twenty-two years investigating flying saucers, catalogued 12,618 sightings, and left 701 unexplained. That is a real number, and it means less than it sounds.

This is the one topic in the archive where the official record is genuinely interesting. Unlike hollow planets, UFOs are a documented administrative fact: there were offices, budgets, files, and a chain of command. The question is not whether the government looked. It is what it found.

Project Blue Book

From 1947 to 1969 the US Air Force investigated unidentified flying objects, from 1952 under the name Project Blue Book, headquartered at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base in Ohio. When it closed on 17 December 1969, the totals were:

  • 12,618 sightings reported
  • 701 left “unidentified”

Those figures are on the National Archives’ own pages and in the Air Force fact sheet. The closure followed a University of Colorado study — the “Condon Report” — reviewed by the National Academy of Sciences, which concluded that further investigation was not justified. The archives are public.

The crucial word is “unidentified”. It means the report could not be resolved with the data available — not that an extraterrestrial craft was confirmed. Most cases were aircraft, balloons, satellites, weather phenomena, birds, or hoaxes; the residue is thin, old, and often missing its original instrument data.

Roswell, in the record

The story starts with a press release. On 8 July 1947 the Roswell Army Air Field public information office announced the recovery of a “flying disc”. The next day the commanding general of the Eighth Air Force said it was a radar-tracking balloon. The newspaper survived; the correction was less exciting.

In 1994 and 1997 the Air Force published two reports on the incident, and the GAO ran its own records search in 1995. The conclusion, in the Air Force’s words:

“The materials recovered near Roswell were consistent with a balloon device of the type used in a then-classified project.”US Air Force, The Roswell Report; see also the National Archives

The classified project was Mogul — balloon trains carrying microphones to detect Soviet nuclear tests. It explains the strange debris and the secrecy. The “alien bodies” have a separate and rather sad explanation: the reports match anthropomorphic test dummies flown on high-altitude balloon experiments years later, and in some cases airmen killed in actual accidents. The Air Force report notes that events spread over many years have been consolidated in the retelling into two or three days in July 1947.

The GAO found no records of a crashed extraterrestrial craft, including in material that had previously been withheld for security reasons.

The modern cases

Two things changed after 2004.

The Nimitz encounter. On 14 November 2004, two F/A-18s from the USS Nimitz were redirected to investigate radar contacts off San Diego. Pilots reported a white, tic-tac-shaped object with no wings, no exhaust, and motion their systems could not lock. Gun-camera footage was later released and authenticated. It remains unexplained in the sense that no public analysis has closed it — the candidate explanations (instrument artefacts, radar spoofing, a classified drone) each have problems.

The programme. In December 2017 the New York Times revealed the Advanced Aerospace Threat Identification Program, a $22 million Pentagon effort run partly at the request of Senator Harry Reid, and the subsequent official structures: the UAP Task Force, then AARO, the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office.

In June 2021 the Office of the Director of National Intelligence published a preliminary assessment covering 144 reports. It concluded that most could be explained, that 143 remained unexplained for lack of data, and — importantly — that there was no evidence linking them to extraterrestrial technology. AARO’s 2024 historical report goes further, reviewing the whole post-1945 record and finding no verified extraterrestrial artefacts or reverse-engineering programme.

How to hold all of this at once

Three statements are simultaneously true. (1) Some sightings are genuinely unidentified, including ones with military sensor data. (2) The historical record contains no verified extraterrestrial object. (3) Governments do classify things, and have lied about programmes — which is why “the government said so” is not a sufficient answer either.

The honest position is the boring one: unidentified is a status, not a conclusion, and the people doing the identifying are the first to say so.

Project Blue Beam: the conspiracy that explains all the others

Project Blue Beam is the claim that a coalition of governments and NASA intends to use holographic projections, lasers and low-frequency radio to stage a fake alien invasion — or a fake religious second coming — and use the resulting panic to install a single world government.

It was popularised in the 1990s by the Canadian journalist Serge Monast, and it has a structural property that makes it almost impossible to argue with: it predicts a spectacle that has not happened, and it explains the absence of evidence by saying the plan is secret.

Its real function is to pre-empt disconfirmation. If a convincing UFO event occurs, it was Blue Beam. If nothing occurs, the plan is delayed. Every future observation is absorbed. That is the same machine as Last Thursdayism, and the same one the Tartaria story runs — only here the mechanism is a hologram rather than a flood.

Worth noting for honesty: governments have run deception operations and have lied about aircraft and radar many times (see Proven Conspiracies). The reason Blue Beam fails is not that governments are trustworthy. It is that the specific claim — a global holographic projector capable of fooling everyone, kept secret by thousands of people — has no evidence and cannot be falsified.
