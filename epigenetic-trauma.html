Epigenetic Inheritance of Trauma

The claim that trauma leaves chemical marks on DNA that pass to children and grandchildren. The human studies are small and confounded; the mechanism is real in animals and unproven in people.

The claim

Epigenetic inheritance holds that environmental experience — famine, stress, trauma — alters DNA methylation or histone marks in a way that is transmitted to offspring, changing their physiology or behaviour without changing their genes. The Dutch Hunger Winter and the descendants of Holocaust survivors are the most cited human cases.

Maps, diagrams and images

The evidence

The animal evidence is stronger: in mice, odour-fear conditioning and stress paradigms have produced effects in offspring, with some molecular correlates. The human evidence is weaker. The Dutch Hunger Winter cohort shows health differences by gestational timing, which is real; the methylation differences reported in descendants come from small samples and have not been consistently replicated.

The central biological problem is germline reprogramming: mammalian embryos erase most epigenetic marks shortly after fertilisation, which is why transgenerational inheritance of acquired marks is hard to explain mechanistically.

Where it stands

Documented: epigenetic marks respond to environment within a lifetime.

Documented: some transgenerational effects in animals.

Contested: transgenerational effects in humans; small, confounded studies.

Not established: a mechanism that survives germline reprogramming in humans.

The honest label: live debate — a real mechanism with an over-extended human claim.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is one of the most emotionally compelling scientific claims of the last twenty years, and the evidence does not yet carry the weight the writing puts on it.
