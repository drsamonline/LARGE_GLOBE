Why We Dream

Dreams are vivid, emotional, remembered badly, and unexplained. The leading theories disagree about whether they do anything at all.

The theories

Hobson's activation-synthesis model treats dreams as the cortex making sense of random brainstem activity during REM — a by-product, not a function. Revonsuo's threat simulation theory argues that dreams rehearse dangers, which would be adaptive. Memory-consolidation accounts note that sleep consolidates, and that dream content often reflects recent experience. Social simulation theories say dreams rehearse relationships.

The evidence

Each has support and none is decisive. Threat simulation predicts more threatening dreams in people exposed to danger, which has been found in some studies and not others. Memory accounts fit the incorporation of recent experience into dreams, which is well documented. The by-product account is hard to falsify and hard to rule out.

The methodological problem is fundamental: dream content is known only from waking reports, which are reconstructions subject to forgetting and expectation.

Where it stands

Documented: dreams occur, mostly in REM, and incorporate recent experience.

Supported: memory-related content; emotional salience.

Contested: whether dreams have a function at all.

Not established: any single function, or any predictive content.

The honest label: live debate, with the by-product hypothesis as the null.

Why it is in the archive

Because dreaming is where the archive's fringe claims (prophecy, shared dreams, astral travel) attach, and the honest scientific position is that the basic function is still unknown.
