The Singularity

The claim that machine intelligence will improve itself in a runaway loop, and that the curve becomes vertical at some point. It rests on an argument about recursive self-improvement that has never been demonstrated.

The argument

I. J. Good described an 'intelligence explosion' in 1965: a machine that can improve its own design would enter a loop in which each generation is better at making the next. Vernor Vinge named the singularity in 1993, and Ray Kurzweil popularised a version with a date attached.

Maps, diagrams and images

The structure is a positive feedback argument: if improvement is proportional to intelligence, and intelligence can be applied to the problem of improving intelligence, the growth is superexponential. Nothing about the argument is irrational; the question is whether its premises hold.

The problems

Every step has friction. Improving a system requires compute, data and experiments, and returns on those are diminishing. Recursive self-improvement also requires the system to understand its own architecture better than its designers do, which is not the same as being good at tasks.

The dated versions have failed: predictions of a singularity by 2020 or 2030 have passed or are passing without a general system that improves itself. The undated version remains a possibility rather than a forecast.

Where it stands

Documented: the argument, its history, and its influence on AI research and policy.

Not demonstrated: recursive self-improvement in any system.

Failed: the dated predictions.

Contested: whether the feedback loop is possible at all, given compute and data limits.

The honest label: fringe — a serious argument about a possible future, with no evidence that the mechanism operates.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is the most consequential unfalsifiable claim in the collection, and because the difference between 'this could happen' and 'this is happening' is where most of the popular writing lives.
