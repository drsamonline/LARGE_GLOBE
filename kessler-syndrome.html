Kessler Syndrome

Enough collisions in low orbit could produce a cascade that makes the region unusable for centuries. The mechanism is real; whether the cascade has started is argued.

The mechanism

Donald Kessler proposed in 1978 that a sufficiently dense population of orbital debris would begin a self-sustaining cascade: each collision creates fragments that raise the chance of further collisions. Above a threshold, the process continues without any new launches.

Maps, diagrams and images

The physics is not in doubt. A fleck of paint at orbital velocity carries the energy of a bullet, and a collision between two intact satellites produces thousands of trackable fragments.

The evidence

The 2007 Chinese anti-satellite test and the 2009 Iridium–Cosmos collision each added thousands of trackable objects, and the population has grown since. Modelling suggests parts of low orbit are already in a slow cascade for some size classes, and that mitigation — deorbiting satellites within 25 years, then 5 — can stabilise the environment if it is done consistently.

The megaconstellation era complicates the arithmetic: thousands of satellites raise both the collision risk and the operational incentive to deorbit them.

Where it stands

Documented: the mechanism, the growth in debris, and the collision events.

Documented: mitigation can stabilise the environment if applied.

Contested: whether a cascade has already begun in some orbits.

Contested: how megaconstellations change the risk.

The honest label: live debate — a real mechanism with a threshold nobody can measure precisely.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is a claim about a threshold that is neither alarmism nor reassurance, and because the numbers are public in a way that most of the archive's subjects are not.
