The Green Run

A secret test released radioactive material over Washington state to see how it would disperse. It was detected 300 km away, and the public was told nothing.

The test

In December 1949, at the Hanford Site in Washington state, the US Atomic Energy Commission conducted a secret test called Green Run: irradiated fuel was processed to release radioactive material — including iodine-131 and xenon-133 — into the atmosphere, to study how it would disperse and to calibrate monitoring for Soviet tests.

The consequences

The plume was detected hundreds of kilometres away, and the release was kept secret for decades. The test is part of the wider record of Hanford's releases, which contaminated the Columbia River and the surrounding area and made the site the largest nuclear cleanup in the United States.

The documents were declassified in the 1980s, and the health effects on downwind communities remain an active subject of study and compensation claims.

What is established

Documented: the test, the release and the detection.

Documented: the secrecy and the later declassification.

Contested: the health effects and their attribution.

Documented: Hanford's wider contamination record.

The label: documented.

Why it is in the archive

Because it is a deliberate release of radiation over a civilian area, done in secret, and documented in the government's own files.
