The Simpsons Predictions

The show has run for more than 750 episodes of jokes about the future. People remember the ones that landed. Here is the whole list, checked episode by episode, with the ones that did not land.

The count first

5Exact hits
4Partial hits
5Coincidences
5Fakes & wrong
2Still awaited

Twenty-one claims, scored below. "Exact" means the episode named a specific thing that later happened. "Partial" means the broad shape matched but a detail did not. "Coincidence" means the scene exists but was not about the future at all. "Fake" means the screenshot was doctored, the clip spliced, or the episode never happened.

Why a 35-year cartoon looks psychic

Four things, in order of how much work they do.

  1. Volume. More than 750 episodes over 35 years contain tens of thousands of jokes about the future. If a show makes a thousand guesses and a hundred are checkable, roughly a handful will look uncanny. "The more unlikely thing would be that we never predicted anything," the showrunner Matt Selman has said.
  2. Vagueness plus retrofitting. A joke about "President Trump" in 2000 became "a prediction" in 2016. A brochure that reads "$9" became "9/11" in 2001. The claim is written after the event, and the episode is re-read to fit it.
  3. Informed guessing. The writers were reading the news: Trump was flirting with a 2000 run, Disney was already buying studios, Holmström was a plausible Nobel pick, and a tiger act was dangerous by definition. Al Jean's summary: predicting FIFA was corrupt is "a very easy prediction".
  4. Selection. Nobody shares the 99% that did not happen. The list you have seen is a highlight reel assembled backwards from the outcomes.

None of this means the coincidences are not strange. The 9/11 brochure genuinely unsettles the people who made it. Strange is not the same as predictive, and a coincidence is not evidence of a hidden script — it is evidence that a long-running show generates a lot of text.

And the films?

The same accounting applies, with one difference: films set a date on screen, which makes them easy to score. Eight of the most-cited cases:

Film count: 2 hits, 5 partial, 1 miss. Notice what the hits have in common — they are satires of trends already visible when they were made, not forecasts of technology.

The honest summary

Of twenty-one famous Simpsons claims, five are exact, four are partly right, five are coincidences read backwards, five are fakes or simply wrong, and two are still waiting. The films score about the same way, and for the same reason: stories are good at extrapolating trends and bad at naming dates.
