Folie à Deux

A delusion shared by two people in a close relationship, usually one dominant and one suggestible. It is rare, it is real, and the modern diagnostic manuals have quietly stopped calling it a separate illness.

The syndrome

Folie à deux was described by Charles Lasègue and Jules Falret in 1877: a delusional belief shared by two people, typically living closely together, with one (the 'inducer') psychotic and the other adopting the belief. The shared content is often persecutory, and separation usually resolves the second person's delusion — which is the strongest evidence that the sharing is social.

The modern term is shared psychotic disorder, and DSM-5 folded it into the schizophrenia spectrum rather than keeping it as its own category. Variants exist for more than two people (folie à trois, and so on) and for the direction of influence.

Why it matters

Because it is the clearest clinical demonstration that beliefs are social objects. The mechanism is ordinary — proximity, dependence, a shared frame — and it scales in ways that are uncomfortable: the same dynamics appear in closed groups, in families, and online.

What is established

Documented: the syndrome, its typical structure, and its resolution on separation.

Documented: it is rare in clinical settings.

Not established: a distinct mechanism. It is described, not explained.

Not established: a general 'contagion' of delusion in crowds, which is a different and weaker claim.

Why it is in the archive: because the archive's subject is claims, and this is the clinical case where a claim is transmitted by relationship rather than by evidence.

Why it is here

Because it is the human-scale version of every group belief the archive documents, and because the treatment — separation, not argument — is a fact worth knowing.
