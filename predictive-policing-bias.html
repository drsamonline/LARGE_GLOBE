Predictive Policing Bias

Systems that send police where crime data says crime happens, which is where police have been sent before.

The problem

Predictive policing systems trained on arrest records learn where arrests happen. Because policing is unevenly distributed, the model sends officers to the same neighbourhoods, generating more arrests there — a documented feedback loop.

Maps, diagrams and images

The evidence

Investigations by journalists and researchers, notably of PredPol and similar systems, documented the circularity, and several cities discontinued programmes. Audits in other jurisdictions found similar problems in risk-scoring tools.

The archive files it as documented.

Why the feedback loop is the finding

Predictive policing models are trained on recorded crime, and recorded crime is produced by police activity. Send more patrols to a district and it generates more recorded incidents, which confirms the model's prediction, which sends more patrols. The model is not measuring crime; it is measuring attention.

The empirical work on this is specific. A study of one large deployment found that the model's predictions did not outperform simple historical baselines, while the patrols it directed concentrated stops in already over-policed neighbourhoods. The accuracy claim and the equity claim are the same claim, and it was not supported.

The archive's entry is labelled a live debate rather than a debunking because the technique is not settled: some deployments use it for resource allocation rather than for individual prediction, which is a different and less harmful use. The problem is the loop, and the loop is avoidable.
